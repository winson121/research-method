WEEK 5 Roadmap — inferential statistics & communicating research

What week 5 covers, the official learning outcomes, and how the two halves (defending claims with statistics / telling people about them) fit your assessments.

Why this week exists

Week 4 taught you to describe data (a quick catch-up for readers who skipped it: the mean = the average, the median = the middle value, the variance = how spread out the values are, and charts = pictures of those summaries). But describing a sample is rarely the goal. In most cases, your quantitative data is a sample of a population, and the question you actually want answered is about the population as a whole. That is the job of inferential statistics — and each inferential test is built for a slightly different scenario and relies on a different set of assumptions about your data. A simple example of an assumption: are you sampling the same group of participants multiple times (within-subjects) or multiple groups once each (between-subjects)? To ask questions of data at all you need a hypothesis — a specific, testable prediction you can check with data — which can only be tested if your experiment was designed correctly — that dependency chain (design → hypothesis → test) is what Part A is about. Part B then asks: once you have a finding, how do you communicate it — to your team, your organisation, or a wider audience — and how do you compress a piece of research into a 90-second video?

The week's learning outcomes (verbatim from the unit page)

  • Understand and implement 1-way ANOVA (analysis of variance — the standard test for comparing the means of three or more groups at once).
  • Recognise non-parametric and parametric tests (the two big families of statistical tests — defined in the next section) and when to apply each.
  • Identify and describe independent, dependent and confounding variables (the suspected cause, the measured outcome, and a hidden third variable that can distort the comparison — all defined below).
  • Be aware of issues surrounding experiment design and choosing an appropriate hypothesis test.
  • Understand why we need to communicate research.
  • Understand the need for clarity and conciseness.
  • Understand the importance of tailoring your message for different audiences.
  • Comment on the structure and production qualities of different content.
  • Create a structure and narrative outline to communicate a piece of existing research.

Every one of these outcomes maps to a section in this module — the outcomes are effectively the quiz's table of contents.

WEEK 5 · PART A The inferential landscape — sample, population, and the test families

Everything from the Part A introduction: what inferential statistics is for, the within/between divide, parametric vs non-parametric, and where each named test sits.

Plain-terms primer — the eight words every later section uses

Read this once and the rest of Part A needs no dictionary. Each term below is re-explained briefly where it is used, but here is the shared foundation.

  • Population — everyone or everything you actually care about ("all users of this app"). You almost never get to measure it. Throughout the module, n = the size of one sample (e.g. n = 7 per group), N = the total number of participants across all groups, and k = the number of groups.
  • Sample — the subset you did measure ("the 14 people who tried the prototype"). Statistics computed on the sample are used to guess about the population.
  • Distribution — the overall shape of a set of values: where they cluster, how spread out they are, whether they trail off to one side.
  • Normal distribution — the symmetric, bell-shaped distribution many natural measurements roughly follow. Parametric tests assume something close to this shape.
  • Variance / spread — how far values typically sit from their average. High variance = wildly different values; low variance = tightly clustered. Two groups with different variances have different "widths" of scatter.
  • Rank — a value's position when the data is sorted smallest to largest (1st, 2nd, 3rd…). Ranks capture order but forget the exact gaps — that is what makes rank tests safe when exact values are unreliable.
  • Nominal scale — categories with no order at all: colours, departments, yes/no labels. Not even ranking is possible. Ordinal scale — data with a meaningful order but no meaningful arithmetic: letter grades (A > B), satisfaction ratings, race positions. You can rank ordinal data but you cannot meaningfully average it. Interval/ratio scale — true numbers (seconds, counts, temperatures) where arithmetic like the mean makes sense; "continuous" simply means such measured numbers rather than counts.
  • Dependent vs independent samples — dependent means the two measurements belong together in pairs (the same person twice); independent means the two groups share no one (different people in each group). This single fact decides half the test choices in this module.

Sample vs population — the whole point of the week

The Part A introduction states the situation precisely: in most cases, most quantitative data represents a sample of a population, and you actually want to understand (or ask a question about) the characteristics of the population as a whole. Inferential statistics are designed to do just that — but each test is designed for slightly different scenarios and relies on a different set of assumptions about your data. Two consequences the quiz tests directly:

1 · The test must match the design

The same two datasets of completion times call for different tests depending on how they were collected — same 8 people on two interfaces (dependent) vs 8 people on one interface and 8 different people on the other (independent). Design first, test second.

2 · Assumptions are checkable facts, not fine print

Normality, scale of measurement (nominal/ordinal/interval-ratio), equal variances, independence — each test lists its assumptions, and violating them is exactly what the non-parametric alternatives are for.

WEEK 5 · PART A Hypothesis testing decoded — hypotheses, errors, and the decision

The hypothesis-testing machinery the workshop's opening discussion covers: why hypotheses matter, the testing steps, the two error types, tails, and the variables (independent / dependent / confounding) that the design is built from.

Why hypothesis testing matters

Sample data is noisy: two groups will almost never have exactly the same mean even if nothing real is going on. Hypothesis testing is the disciplined answer to "is this difference real, or could it plausibly be chance?" It works by assuming there is no real effect (the null hypothesis), working out how surprising your data would be under that assumption, and rejecting the assumption only if the surprise is too big to swallow. Workshop 5A opens with exactly this discussion — the importance of hypothesis testing and the statistical hypothesis testing steps — before any computation.

The vocabulary — one of each

Plain-terms glosses. Standard error = the typical size of "how far off" a sample average would be if you repeated the study — the ruler's margin of error, in the same units as the data. Degrees of freedom (df) = how many values in your data are still free to vary once you've pinned down the averages you computed from them; it indexes the correct row of the critical-value table. Test statistic = the whole dataset compressed into one number so a table can judge it: observed difference ÷ typical random fluctuation. α = your pre-declared tolerance for crying wolf: the long-run fraction of times this procedure will declare a difference that isn't there.

Why ANOVA exists (the unit's own argument): if you have more than 2 groups and run a t-test on every pair at α = .05, each test has a 5% false-positive rate; across many pairs the chance of at least one spurious "significant" result balloons. ANOVA tests all groups in one test at the stated α — which is why "run three t-tests instead" is a wrong answer on the quiz, and why a significant ANOVA is followed by post-hoc tests that control the family-wise error (the combined false-positive chance across the whole family of pairwise tests).

One-tailed vs two-tailed

  • Two-tailed (default): H₁ says "different", direction unknown. All the hand-tests in this module use two-tailed critical values unless told otherwise.
  • One-tailed: H₁ predicts a direction ("faster", "lower"). It must be chosen before seeing the data — peeking and then choosing a tail doubles your real α.
  • Consequence: a one-tailed test at the same α has a lower critical value (easier to reach) — that's exactly why choosing it post-hoc is cheating.

WEEK 5 · PART A The t-test family — Student, Welch, and the assumption audit

Deep dive on the parametric two-group tests: what the pooled t-test from week 4 assumes, why Welch's variant exists, and how to audit any test's assumptions before trusting it.

From week 4 to week 5 — recapping the pooled t-test in one breath

Week 4's workshop computed a two-sample (Student, pooled) t-test by hand — carried forward here as a reminder, with its full derivation in the week-4 module: pool the two group variances into sp², get the standard error, form t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/SE (x̄ = a group's mean; n₁, n₂ = the two group sizes), and compare |t| with the critical value at df = n₁ + n₂ − 2. Its result on the week-4 typing-speed data was t(22) = −3.02, p = .006. That test quietly assumed two things beyond normality: independent groups and roughly equal variances. Week 5's question: what happens when those assumptions fail?

Welch's t-test — the unit's named answer to unequal variances

The unit page defines it exactly: "Welch's T-test: a T-Test style method which is suited for data where samples have unequal variances and/or unequal sample sizes." The difference from Student's version is in the denominator and the degrees of freedom:

  • Student (pooled): SE = √( sp²·(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ) with sp² a weighted average of the two variances; df = n₁ + n₂ − 2. One shared variance estimate — fine when the groups genuinely have similar spread.
  • Welch (unpooled): SE = √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) — each group keeps its own variance; df is approximated by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula (smaller than n₁+n₂−2 when variances differ). No pooling = no pooling assumption.
  • Practical guidance (canon): when n's are similar and variances within a ratio of ~2, Student and Welch give nearly identical answers; when variances or sample sizes are clearly unequal, Welch is the safer default — it controls Type I error properly where the pooled test inflates it.
  • MATLAB (the numerical-computing software used in the unit's workshops): [h,p] = ttest2(g1,g2,'Vartype','unequal') runs Welch; the default 'equal' runs Student.

The assumption audit — three questions before any test

  1. How many groups/conditions? Two → t-family or two-sample rank tests. Three or more → ANOVA (don't stack t-tests).
  2. Same people or different people? Same people measured twice → paired/dependent tests (paired t, or Wilcoxon signed-rank if not normal). Different people → independent-group tests (Student/Welch t, or Mann-Whitney U if ordinal/not normal).
  3. Is the data roughly normal (and what scale)? Interval/ratio and plausibly normal → parametric. Ordinal (ordered, no arithmetic), skewed (lopsided with one long tail), outlier-heavy or small-n and non-normal → non-parametric on ranks. The week's one-line definitions of Mann-Whitney ("ordinal or continuous, but not normally distributed") and Wilcoxon ("cannot assume normally distributed… 2 dependent samples") are exactly these audit questions answered.

Side note for assignment work: with tiny samples (the workshop's n = 7 or n = 8) normality is essentially unverifiable — which is precisely why the workshop routes you to the rank-based tests for the letter-scale and small-sample data. Small n + unknown distribution → ranks.

Why ranks rescue you — the idea behind both non-parametric tests

Converting values to ranks (1st smallest, 2nd smallest, …) throws away the raw magnitudes — an A and a B might be 1 point or 10 points apart on the underlying scale, but ranks don't care. That sounds like a loss (and it is: less power when the data was normal all along), but it buys three things: (1) no normality assumption — ranks are always "well-behaved"; (2) ordinal data works — A–E ratings have no meaningful arithmetic but a perfect ordering; (3) outliers can't dominate — one wild value becomes one rank, not a lever arm. Mann-Whitney U ranks across two independent groups; Wilcoxon signed-rank ranks the sizes of within-person differences. Same trick, two designs.

Compact worked example — Student's t on fresh data (the week-4 pipeline, end to end)

The scenario. Two different groups of 6 participants each complete a puzzle; seconds to solve. Group 1 (hint available): 52, 55, 58, 54, 56, 53 · Group 2 (no hint): 48, 50, 47, 51, 49, 46. Times look roughly normal with similar spread — the pooled t-test's home ground.

Group statistics. x̄₁ = 54.67, s₁² = 4.67 · x̄₂ = 48.5, s₂² = 3.50. (Each s² uses the sample divisor n−1 = 5.)

Pool. sp² = [5(4.67) + 5(3.50)]/10 = 4.08.

Standard error. SE = √(4.08 × (1/6 + 1/6)) = √(1.3611) = 1.167.

t. t = (54.67 − 48.5)/1.167 = 6.167/1.167 = 5.29, df = 6 + 6 − 2 = 10.

Decision. Two-tailed crit (df 10) = 2.23 at α = .05 → reject H₀ (p ≈ .0004, where ≈ means "approximately"): the hint group solved significantly faster. One-line report: t(10) = 5.29, p < .001, two-tailed. Had the spreads differed clearly (say 4.7 vs 12), the same arithmetic with unpooled variances would be Welch — that choice happens BEFORE computing, at the assumption audit.

n₁ = n₂ = 7. Ratings are ordinal (A beats B, but "by how much" is undefined) and n is tiny — this is exactly the unit's Mann-Whitney scenario: "two groups, dependent variable ordinal or continuous but not normally distributed".

Step 0 · The assumptions (answer to part 1)

  • The two samples are independent — different people in each group (violating this is the most common wrong-test choice: same people → Wilcoxon signed-rank instead).
  • The dependent variable is at least ordinal — a ranking of the values must be meaningful (A < B < C… is; favourite colours aren't).
  • The distributions are similarly shaped if you want to interpret a significant result as a difference in medians; the test itself only needs the rankings. (Canon wording — the workshop slide procedure.)
  • No normality assumption — that is the point of the test.

Step 0b · The hypotheses (answer to part 2)

H₀: the two groups' ratings have the same distribution (their population medians are equal — the median being the middle value, the point where half the ratings are better and half worse; ordinal data has a median but no meaningful mean). H₁ (two-tailed): the groups' ratings differ. α = 0.05. Note the null is about identical distributions, not about equal means — means don't exist on an ordinal scale.

Steps 1–5 · The full hand computation

The complete working, written out (so you can copy the layout in the workshop)

1 · Pool and order all 14 values. A A A B B B B B C C C C D D.

2 · Assign ranks; average ties. The three A's occupy ranks 1–3 → each gets (1+2+3)/3 = 2. The five B's occupy ranks 4–8 → each gets (4+5+6+7+8)/5 = 6. The four C's occupy ranks 9–12 → each gets (9+10+11+12)/4 = 10.5. The two D's occupy ranks 13–14 → each gets (13+14)/2 = 13.5.

3 · Sum each group's ranks. Group 1 (A B A B B C D): 2+6+2+6+6+10.5+13.5 = R₁ = 46. Group 2 (A C C B D C B): 2+10.5+10.5+6+13.5+10.5+6 = R₂ = 59. Check: 46 + 59 = 105 = 14×15/2 ✓ (if this fails, a rank is wrong).

4 · Compute U for each group. U₁ = n₁n₂ + n₁(n₁+1)/2 − R₁ = 49 + 28 − 46 = 31. U₂ = n₁n₂ + n₂(n₂+1)/2 − R₂ = 49 + 28 − 59 = 18. Check: U₁ + U₂ = n₁n₂ = 49 ✓ (31 + 18 = 49).

5 · The test statistic U = min(U₁, U₂) = 18.

6 · Critical value from the Mann-Whitney table for n₁ = n₂ = 7, two-tailed α = .05: U crit = 8 (textbook table). Reject H₀ only if U ≤ 8 (≤ means "less than or equal to" — and note this test rejects at SMALL values, the reverse of t and F).

7 · Decision. U = 18 > 8 → fail to reject H₀. There is no significant difference between the groups' ratings (the exact p-value is ≈ .45 — far from significance, so this is a comfortable non-rejection, not a borderline one).

Result statement (answer to part 4 — the exam-style sentence)

"A Mann-Whitney U test found no significant difference between the two groups' ratings, U = 18, n₁ = n₂ = 7, p > .05 (critical U = 8 at α = .05, two-tailed). The null hypothesis is retained: the data does not support a difference in how the two groups were rated."

The traps the quiz sets on this procedure

  • Wrong statistic: using U₁ = 31 instead of min(U₁, U₂) = 18. The test statistic is always the smaller U (tables are built that way).
  • Ties ignored: giving every A rank 1 (or ranking ties 1,2,3) instead of averaged ranks 2. With ties everywhere in this dataset, that corrupts both rank sums.
  • Decision reversed: Mann-Whitney tables reject when U is small (U ≤ critical), the opposite of t and F where big is significant. A quick sanity check: if U is near n₁n₂/2 (here 24.5), the groups are interleaved — no difference.
  • Wrong test family: choosing a t-test because "there are two groups" — the data is ordinal with n = 7 per group; the assumptions fail by construction, which is the lesson's whole point.

Worked example 2 — numeric ratings, decided by one ranking pass

The scenario. Two independent groups of 5 participants rate a prototype's ease of use from 1 (worst) to 10 (best). Group 1: 8, 6, 9, 7, 5 · Group 2: 2, 3, 4, 3, 5. Ratings are treated as ordinal, so Mann-Whitney U applies.

Step 1 · Pool and rank. Combined, in order: 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. Ranks with tie-averaging: 2→1 · the two 3s share (2+3)/2 = 2.5 · 4→4 · the two 5s share 5.5 · 6→7 · 7→8 · 8→9 · 9→10.

Step 2 · Rank sums. Group 1: 9+7+10+8+5.5 = R₁ = 39.5 · Group 2: 1+2.5+4+2.5+5.5 = R₂ = 15.5. Check: 39.5 + 15.5 = 55 = 10×11/2 ✓.

Step 3 · U values. U₁ = 25 + 15 − 39.5 = 0.5 · U₂ = 25 + 15 − 15.5 = 24.5. Check: 0.5 + 24.5 = 25 = 5×5 ✓.

Step 4 · Decision. U = min = 0.5. Critical value for n₁ = n₂ = 5, two-tailed α = .05 is 2 (Siegel & Castellan Table L). 0.5 ≤ 2 → reject H₀: Group 1 rated the prototype significantly higher (exact p ≈ .016). Note how extreme 0.5 is: it sits almost at the theoretical minimum of 0, meaning Group 1 owned nearly every top rank.

Step 0 · The assumptions (answer to part 1)

  • The two samples are dependent — paired measurements on the same subjects (here: each person did both interfaces).
  • The dependent variable is at least ordinal — differences can be ordered by size.
  • The distribution of the differences is symmetric about their median — mirror-shaped, so positive and negative differences of the same size are equally likely (canon wording). This is what licenses interpreting a significant result as a shift in the median difference.
  • No normality assumption on the raw values.

Steps 1–6 · The full hand computation

The complete working, written out

1 · Difference each pair (Interface 1 − Interface 2): −20, −20, 0, −15, −15, −10, −10, −15. Person 3's difference is exactly 0 and is dropped (cannot carry a sign) → effective n = 7.

2 · Rank the absolute differences. |d| values: 20, 20, 15, 15, 15, 10, 10. The two 20s take ranks 6–7 → 6.5 each. The three 15s take ranks 3–5 → 4 each. The two 10s take ranks 1–2 → 1.5 each.

3 · Reattach signs and sum. Every non-zero difference is negative (Interface 2 always slower or equal). W⁺ (positive ranks) = 0. W⁻ (negative ranks) = 6.5+6.5+4+4+4+1.5+1.5 = 28. Check: W⁺ + W⁻ = n(n+1)/2 = 7×8/2 = 28 ✓.

4 · Test statistic W = min(W⁺, W⁻) = 0.

5 · Critical value from the Wilcoxon signed-rank table for n = 7, two-tailed α = .05: T crit = 2 (textbook table). Reject H₀ if W ≤ 2.

6 · Decision. W = 0 ≤ 2 → reject H₀. The two interfaces differ significantly; the direction is unanimous — Interface 2 is slower for every person who differed (the data's exact p-value ≈ .016).

Result statement (answer to part 4)

"A Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed task-completion times differed significantly between the two interfaces, W = 0, n = 7 (after dropping one zero difference), p < .05 (critical W = 2 at α = .05, two-tailed). Signed ranks were unanimous in direction: Interface 2 was slower for every participant who recorded a difference."

Worked example 2 — before/after training, and the near-miss that teaches the most

The scenario. Six call-centre staff are timed (seconds) on a task before and after a training session — the same six people, so the samples are dependent. Before: 30, 28, 35, 40, 33, 29 · After: 22, 30, 25, 33, 28, 24. The differences look systematically positive (most people faster after), but the sample is tiny — exactly the situation Wilcoxon handles.

Step 1 · Differences (before − after). 8, −2, 10, 7, 5, 5. No zeros, so n = 6.

Step 2 · Rank |d| with tie-averaging. |d|: 8, 2, 10, 7, 5, 5 → 2 gets 1 · the two 5s share 2.5 · 7 gets 4 · 8 gets 5 · 10 gets 6.

Step 3 · Signed sums. Only one difference is negative (−2), so W⁺ = 5+6+4+2.5+2.5 = 20 and W⁻ = 1. Check: 20 + 1 = 21 = 6×7/2 ✓.

Step 4 · Decision. W = min(20, 1) = 1. Critical value for n = 6, two-tailed α = .05 is 0 (Siegel & Castellan Table J). 1 > 0 → fail to reject H₀ — just barely (exact p = .063).

The lesson. With n = 6 you need PERFECT unanimity: even one dissenting pair (−2) was enough to miss significance at .05. Small-sample rank tests are honest but blunt instruments — a non-rejection here means "not proven", not "no effect". This is the same logic that made Lesson B's W = 0 significant: zero dissenters.

Step 1 · Hypotheses and group statistics

H₀: μ_programmers = μ_pilots = μ_police (μ, pronounced "mu", is the standard symbol for a population mean) — all three professions have the same mean task-completion time. H₁: at least one profession's mean differs (not all equal — ANOVA does not say which). α = 0.05.

Group means: Programmers 820/5 = 164.0 · Pilots 945/8 = 118.125 · Police 960/7 ≈ 137.14. Grand mean (all 20 values): 2725/20 = 136.25. Note the unequal group sizes — ANOVA handles them; that's one of its jobs.

Steps 2–7 · The full hand computation

The complete working, written out

2 · Sums of squares between (SSB) = Σ nᵢ(meanᵢ − grand)² (Σ, a capital Greek sigma, simply means "sum of" — add one such term per group) = 5(164 − 136.25)² + 8(118.125 − 136.25)² + 7(137.143 − 136.25)² = 5(770.06) + 8(328.52) + 7(0.80) = 3850.31 + 2628.13 + 5.58 = 6484.02.

3 · Sums of squares within (SSW) = Σ over every value of (value − its own group mean)² = 970.00 (Programmers) + 2946.87 (Pilots) + 3142.86 (Police) = 7059.73.

Cross-check via totals: Σx² = 384825 and (Σx)²/N = 2725²/20 = 371281.25, so SST = 384825 − 371281.25 = 13543.75. And SSB + SSW = 6484.02 + 7059.73 = 13543.75 ✓ — the partition closes exactly.

4 · Degrees of freedom: dfB = k − 1 = 2 · dfW = N − k = 17 · dfT = N − 1 = 19.

5 · Mean squares: MSB = 6484.02/2 = 3242.01 · MSW = 7059.73/17 = 415.28.

6 · F statistic: F = MSB/MSW = 3242.01/415.28 = 7.81.

7 · Decision: F-table critical value for df (2, 17) at α = .05 is 3.59. F = 7.81 > 3.59 → reject H₀. (The data's exact p ≈ .004 — comfortably significant.) At least one profession's mean completion time differs.

Plain-terms glosses for the arithmetic. Deviation = one value's distance from a mean (value − mean, in seconds); squaring makes every distance positive so errors can't cancel. Grand mean = the average of ALL 20 values together, ignoring profession. SSB ("between") = how far each group mean sits from the grand mean, weighted by group size — the variation profession can explain. SSW ("within") = how far individuals sit from their own group's mean — the leftover noise. Mean square = a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom (a per-unit version of the variation). F = between-variation per unit ÷ within-variation per unit; F ≈ 1 would mean profession explains nothing beyond noise.

"Which hypothesis do you accept?" — wording the workshop uses

The activity asks "which hypothesis of the 1-way ANOVA do you accept? Do you reject the null hypothesis?" The defensible answer: the evidence supports H₁ (reject H₀) — the means are not all equal. Purists add the footnote that we "fail to reject" rather than "prove" H₁, but since ANOVA's H₁ is only "at least one differs", the follow-up question is immediately: which pair(s)? — that's the post-hoc's job.

Post-hoc · Tukey-Kramer by hand (which pairs actually differ)

ANOVA says "at least one differs"; the post-hoc locates it while controlling the family-wise error that stacking t-tests would create. Using Tukey-Kramer (Tukey's honestly significant difference method, extended by Kramer to handle unequal n): for each pair compute q = |meanᵢ − meanⱼ| ÷ √( MSW/2 · (1/nᵢ + 1/nⱼ) ), and compare with q crit = 3.63 from the studentized-range distribution (a special table built for comparing many means at once), with k = 3 groups, df = 17, α = .05.

WEEK 5 · PART B Communicating research — why, to whom, and how

Why communicate at all

The Part B introduction is blunt: "We have covered planning, executing and analysing the results of a research question, but this is all meaningless until you disseminate that research to someone else." A finding no one hears about changes nothing. Research communication is therefore not an optional extra bolted onto the end — it is the step that makes the other steps matter, and it is a skill in its own right: the week's outcomes are about clarity, conciseness, tailoring, and being able to comment on structure and production qualities (editing, sound, pacing, image quality) of content.

Tailoring the message — the three dials

The introduction names exactly what you adjust for each audience: "alternative terminology, removing assumptions about pre-existing knowledge, or adjust the depth to which you talk about the work."

  • Terminology dial: "effect size" to your team; "how big the improvement is" to a stakeholder.
  • Knowledge-assumption dial: never assume the audience knows your acronym, method or prior result — one sentence of context beats five minutes of confusion.
  • Depth dial: the same finding can be a number on a slide, a paragraph in a report, or a 90-second story. Choosing the depth for the audience is the skill — not the depth that shows off your work.

Clarity and conciseness — the two virtues the outcomes name

Clarity

One message, plainly said, that survives being heard once. Test: could your audience repeat your main point to someone else right now? The video script activity (Workshop 5B Activity 4) trains exactly this — a timed, spoken draft forces you to hear your own clutter.

Conciseness

Cut until it hurts, then cut once more. In a 90-second video you get maybe 6–8 segments — every word competes with every other word. "Avoid complicated jargon and as many technical details as necessary" (the worksheet's phrasing for scripts).

WEEK 5 · PART B The 90-second video — beats, arc, audio, script

The craft the studio trains: the value of audio, the 15–20-second beat structure, the narrative arc, expositor choice, and writing dialogue that fits its time slot.

The constraint that shapes everything

"Most videos are punctuated in 15–20 second chunks (beats). This is not a long time, and you might have only 6–8 of these segments in a 90 second video." Do the arithmetic: 90 ÷ 15 = 6 and 90 ÷ 20 ≈ 4.5 — so at the worksheet's quoted 15–20 seconds per beat, only 4.5–6 beats fit; the worksheet nonetheless plans 6–8, which works only if beats run shorter (about 11–15 seconds). What the quiz can quote is the worksheet's own pair of numbers (15–20-second chunks, 6–8 segments); what you do in practice is budget each beat at 12–15 seconds — the model treatment below uses exactly that. Either way the planning lesson stands: a 90-second video has room for only a handful of beats. The beat is the budgeting unit of the whole video: if an idea can't fit a beat, it doesn't fit the video.

The narrative arc — bend the beats into a story

  • The worksheet asks you to "reflect on your story so far in terms of the narrative arc (e.g. inciting incident, falling action, resolution)". Those are storytelling terms: the inciting incident is the event that kicks off the story (here: the problem), falling action is the winding down after the climax (the evidence and consequences), and the resolution is where the story lands (what it means for the audience).
  • A research-paper arc that works in 6–8 beats: (1) the world & the problem it has → (2) why the problem matters / who it hurts → (3) what researchers tried before and why it fell short → (4) the paper's key idea, in one sentence → (5) the evidence it works → (6) what changes because of it → (7–8) what it means for this audience, closing line.
  • The worksheet's own decomposition list: "introduction, background, problem space, approach, how this research will change the world, what it means for the audience" — the same arc from the other side.
  • "There is no right answer" for the shape — but there is a test: every beat should make the next one feel necessary.

Who speaks? — the expositor decision

For each beat, the worksheet asks "'who' will the main expositor of the content — e.g. will it be a narrator, will it be someone being interviewed…". The choice changes the script: the expositor (whoever delivers a beat's content — narrator, interviewee, or on-screen text) shapes how it lands: a narrator controls pacing precisely (best for dense beats); an interviewee brings authenticity and spontaneity (best for the "why it matters" beats); on-screen text carries names and numbers the ear would lose. Strong videos mix expositors deliberately rather than defaulting to one.

The value of audio — "the bedrock of your communication plan"

Workshop 5B Activity 1 makes the point physically: your group is given an image and must communicate it to the class in 1 minute without the class seeing it. The worksheet's claim: "the 'audio' channel of information can transmit a huge amount of information and in many cases should form the bedrock of your communication plan." The debrief questions push at exactly what the exercise exposes — what would have helped you describe it, what descriptive-language strategies worked, what visual elements (other than the object) would have helped. Translation to video: if the audio track alone carries your whole message, your video survives small screens, distractions and accessibility needs; if the audio is an afterthought, the video dies muted.

Script and dialog — writing for the mouth and the clock

  • Workshop Activity 4: take one beat you will personally speak, draft its dialog, then "talk aloud and time each other" — most people discover they have overestimated how much fits (at a natural speaking pace a beat holds roughly 30–55 words, fewer for shorter beats).
  • "In general, try to avoid complicated jargon and as many technical details as necessary" — say the mechanism, not the datasheet; a term earns its place only if the sentence is impossible without it.
  • Iterate the way the worksheet says: if you overrun, "should you reduce the text, and say the same thing in a more concise way?" — compress meaning, don't accelerate speech; talking faster past the timer is the failure mode the activity is designed to catch.
  • The video-treatment template's Justification column forces you to defend every choice "refer to audience, and choice of content and visuals" — script decisions are audience decisions.

Activity 1 · The Value of Audio — model approach and debrief answers

Plan pattern that works: name the category first ("this is a photograph of a street scene at night") — a frame the listener can hang details on; then describe relationships, not pixels ("a dog sits beside a parked bicycle, both lit by a single shop sign"); then one memorable anchor detail; then invite the one question that would resolve the most uncertainty. Keeping under 1 minute means roughly 130–160 words — so rank details before speaking.

Debrief answers (the guide's three questions): What would have helped? — a shared reference frame (real-world scale, category, spatial layout left-to-right). Better descriptive strategies? — comparisons to known objects, relationships over lists, verbs over adjectives. What other visual elements would have helped? — gestures/diagrams drawn live, or a handout: anything that converts a linear audio stream into a spatial one. That is the lesson: audio alone must do sequencing, framing and emphasis — which is why it deserves to be planned first ("the bedrock").

Activity 2 · Dissecting the paper's argument — the four questions, answered on any paper

The guide's questionHow to answer it (the reusable method)
"What is the core argument of this article? Can you identify the argument from the text you read, and how/where?"The argument lives in the abstract's final 1–2 sentences and is restated in the conclusion's opening — find the claim that names what the authors did and why it beats the alternative. If you can prefix it with "We show that…", you've found it.
"Can you identify how this research could be relevant to the audience?"Complete "this matters to [audience] because [their problem it changes]". For a general audience, relevance is rarely the method — it's what the method enables (translation apps, self-driving cars, medical scans).
"What justifications are provided… evidence and logical reasons? Do the justifications support the argument well?"List the evidence types (benchmark numbers, comparisons to the prior state of the art — the best result previously published — plus theoretical arguments) and check each supports this claim — not a neighbouring one. Strong papers quantify ("top-5 error 17.0%" — meaning the correct answer was among the model's five best guesses 83% of the time — beats a vague "works well").
"How much additional context is required for an audience to understand the importance of this work?"Count the concepts a layperson needs before the claim lands. If >2, the first beat of your video is context, not content — budget for it (this is exactly the "background" beat in the arc).

Digests 1–5

1 · Reducing the Dimensionality of Data with Neural Networks (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, Science 2006)

Core argument: high-dimensional data can be compressed into low-dimensional codes by training a multilayer network with a small central layer to reconstruct its inputs — and with the right initialisation, these "autoencoder" networks learn codes that work much better than principal components analysis (PCA). Evidence: the reconstruction/structure quality of the learned low-dimensional representations versus PCA on benchmark data. Audience hook: "how do you compress a million-pixel image into two numbers without losing its shape?" Context needed: what PCA already does; why linear methods fail on curved (manifold) data.

2 · ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, CACM 2017)

Core argument: a large, deep convolutional network — five convolutional + three fully connected layers, 60 million parameters, 650,000 neurons — trained on 1.2 million ImageNet images with GPUs, non-saturating neurons and dropout, classifies far better than anything before. Evidence: error rates of 37.5% (top-1: the single best guess was wrong 37.5% of the time) and 17.0% (top-5: the correct answer was missing from the five best guesses only 17% of the time) versus the previous state of the art; the epilogue notes that by 2015 better hardware and deeper nets cut the error rate a further factor of three, approaching human performance, and credits the large labelled dataset as "the essential missing ingredient". Audience hook: the 2012 moment computers began to see. Context needed: what image classification is; roughly why depth helps.

3 · Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020)

Core argument: diffusion probabilistic models — latent-variable models inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics — can generate high-quality images, trained via a weighted variational bound connected to denoising score matching with Langevin dynamics. Evidence: on unconditional CIFAR-10 (a standard 32×32 photo benchmark) an Inception score of 9.46 and a state-of-the-art FID of 3.17 (two standard image-quality scores — higher Inception / lower FID is better); on 256×256 LSUN, sample quality similar to ProgressiveGAN. Audience hook: how modern image generators learn by learning to un-blur noise. Context needed: what a generative model is; the GAN (see digest 9) it competed with. The conclusion itself flags the broader-impact risk: fake images and videos of public figures.

4 · Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning (Mnih et al., DeepMind 2013)

Core argument: the first deep learning model to learn control policies directly from high-dimensional sensory input — a convolutional network trained with a variant of Q-learning (reward-based learning that values actions by their consequences) whose input is raw pixels and whose output estimates future rewards. Evidence: one architecture, no per-game adjustment, applied to seven Atari 2600 games: state of the art on six, and superhuman on three. The key training trick is experience replay (reusing stored past attempts as practice material) combined with stochastic minibatch updates. Audience hook: the machine that taught itself to play games just by watching the screen. Context needed: what reinforcement learning optimises (rewards); that pixels are an extraordinarily awkward input.

5 · Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: Part I (Durrant-Whyte & Bailey, IEEE RAM 2006)

Core argument: SLAM asks whether a robot dropped in an unknown place can incrementally build a consistent map of the environment while simultaneously determining its own location within that map — and at the theoretical level this is now a solved problem, demonstrated from indoor robots to underwater and airborne systems. Evidence: the tutorial walks the probabilistic formulation and surveys the key implementations and demonstrations. Audience hook: how a vacuum robot or self-driving car can navigate a place nobody has ever mapped. Context needed: the chicken-and-egg loop (needs a map to localise, needs a position to map); why sensors are noisy. Part II (not in your folder) covers the remaining practical issues: computation, data association, features.

Digests 6–10

6 · Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al., 2017)

Core argument: the dominant sequence models of the time were built on recurrence and convolution; this team proposed the Transformer — based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely. Evidence: on two machine-translation tasks the Transformer is superior in quality and trains significantly faster; new state of the art on WMT 2014 English→German and English→French, outperforming even all previously reported ensembles on the former. Audience hook: the architecture behind every modern language model, introduced in one paper. Context needed: roughly what an encoder–decoder is; that translation was the benchmark of the day.

7 · An Image is Worth 16×16 Words (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021 — ViT)

Core argument: the Transformer's dominance in NLP (natural-language processing — text AI) need not stop at text: a pure transformer applied directly to sequences of image patches performs very well on image classification — the reliance on CNNs "is not necessary", when pre-trained on large amounts of data. Evidence: matches or exceeds the state of the art on many image-classification benchmarks while being relatively cheap to pre-train; on smaller datasets CNNs still win (an honesty point the paper concedes). Audience hook: cutting a picture into tiles and treating it like a sentence. Context needed: the Transformer (#6) first; what pre-training is.

8 · Visualizing Data using t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton, JMLR 2008)

Core argument: t-SNE gives each high-dimensional data point a location in a two- or three-dimensional map that reveals structure at many scales, fixing SNE's crowding problem by matching similarity with a heavy-tailed Student-t distribution in the low-dimensional map. Evidence: it outperforms existing techniques on visualising real-world datasets — retaining local structure while revealing global clusters; a landmark variant keeps the O(n²) cost manageable on large data. Audience hook: turning a million-dimensional spreadsheet into a picture where similar things sit together. Context needed: what "high-dimensional" means; nearest-neighbour intuition. (Fun cross-link: the paper's own conclusion notes the reference implementation is in MATLAB.)

9 · Generative Adversarial Nets (Goodfellow et al., 2014)

Core argument: estimate generative models through an adversarial process: train a generator G to capture the data distribution against a discriminator D that estimates the probability a sample is real — a minimax two-player game (one side's gain is exactly the other's loss) with a unique solution where G recovers the training distribution and D cannot beat chance (½ everywhere). Evidence: when G and D are multilayer perceptrons (standard multi-layer neural networks), the whole system trains by backpropagation (the networks' standard learning algorithm — small adjustments propagated backwards from each mistake) with no Markov chains (step-by-step random samplers) or unrolled inference networks — and produces samples the authors judge competitive. Audience hook: a counterfeiter and a detective, training each other. Context needed: what a generative model is; why sampling was previously expensive. The conclusion lists extensions: conditional models, approximate inference, semi-supervised learning, efficiency.

10 · DINOv2: Learning Robust Visual Features without Supervision (Oquab et al., TMLR 2024)

Core argument: following the NLP playbook of pretraining on large data, DINOv2 produces general-purpose visual features that work across image distributions and tasks without fine-tuning (extra training for one specific task after the main training) — trained entirely self-supervised, no labels. Evidence: the curated LVD-142M dataset gives the best balance of image types; scaling data with model size beats training on ImageNet-22k (a ViT-g on LVD-142M matches it on ImageNet-1k and significantly outperforms it elsewhere); ablations quantify the loss components (e.g. KoLeo improves retrieval by more than 8%). Audience hook: a foundation model for images that never needed a single human label. Context needed: what "features" are; the self-supervised idea in one sentence; ImageNet's role as the old benchmark.

A video link dies, moves, or plays without a rewind in the exam. A citation doesn't.
