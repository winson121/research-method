We live in an “interview society”

David Silverman (1993) coined it: interviews are everywhere — online/TV/radio news, talk shows and magazines, documentaries, job seeking. Interviews were once the domain of professional practice; now they “pervade and produce our contemporary cultural experiences and knowledge of our authentic personal, private selves”.

Why researchers care: research interviews leverage the fact that people already know how to “play the game” of presenting themselves through interviews. That cultural fluency is what makes the method work — and also what makes people rehearsed, so a good interviewer works to get past the performance.

Before you interview — the questions to answer first

  1. What is your research topic? What do you wish to explore or explain?
  2. What are your research questions?
  3. What is the purpose of your research?
  4. What might represent knowledge or evidence?

Then two philosophical checkpoints: your epistemological position — epistemology is the study of what does and does not constitute your knowledge; and the ontology of what you study — the concepts and categories in a subject area, their properties and relations. Ontology changes the questions: “sexism” as a study of attitudes asks different questions than sexism as a study of discourse or of institutions.

Advantages of interviews (from the slides)

What interviews win you
  • Flexible — the line of inquiry is modifiable as you learn
  • Benefits from non-verbal communication
  • Gets “behind” the immediate response — the why, versus a questionnaire's what
  • Usable with relatively little prior knowledge of a situation (the typical survey first needs exploratory qualitative work)
  • Situational — captures the participant's view of the world
  • Engaging — often viewed positively by participants
What interviews cost you
  • Not standardised — for semi-structured and unstructured forms
  • Not replicable — for any form of interview
  • Time-consuming (therefore expensive) — typically face-to-face
  • Recruitment can be challenging — time commitment on the participant's side
  • Analysis takes time and effort — transcription, qualitative analysis, disseminating

Types of interview

TypeWho / structureBest when
Structured (respondent interview)Rigid style; researcher has a comprehensive list of questions; no need for follow-up interviewsYou know exactly what you need and want every participant asked identically
Semi-structured (respondent interview)A protocol guides the interview; can incorporate conversational aspects; lots of flexibilityYou have topic areas but want to dig deeper on answers — the default for UX research
Unstructured (informer interview)Few if any prepared questions; progresses like a normal conversation about the research topicSensitive topics; participants' narratives may redirect the conversation away from aspects you anticipated
Other configurationsDebate-based (adversarial) · group-based (focus group) · multiple interviewersSpecial purposes: adversarial scrutiny; interaction effects; workload sharing
Respondent vs informer: respondent interviews ask a participant about themselves; informer interviews ask a key informant about a situation or organisation. Unstructured interviews pair naturally with informers because the informer steers.

The craft rules — what “good practice” means in an interview

Question types to mix in a schedule (from “Strategies for Qualitative Interviews”, Week 3 Moodle)
TypeWhat it doesExample pattern
Opening / rapportPuts the participant at ease; builds trust before substance“Tell me a bit about yourself…” (Sarah asks Vera about board games)
Grand tourBroad open question that lets the participant set the frame“Walk me through your last grocery trip from start to finish.”
StructuralZooms into specific aspects the grand tour surfaced“You mentioned the self-checkout — what happened there?”
ContrastReveals values by comparing two experiences“How is shopping with the app different from going in person?”
Probing follow-upsElaborate, clarify, exemplify: “Tell me more”, “What do you mean by…”, “Can you give an example?”Planned in advance per question — this is what “include potential probes” means
Closing / catch-allInvites what you didn't anticipate; minimises closed questions“Is there anything about … I haven't asked that you think I should know?”
Conduct rules (what the observer is told to watch for in Workshop 3B)
  • Ask, then listen — follow up on answers before moving on; don't robotically march through the schedule.
  • Use the probes you planned — they exist to elicit detail, not to interrogate.
  • One question at a time — no double-barrelled spoken questions either.
  • Neutral reactions — no nodding-along, frowning or “good answer!”; you bias the next answer.
  • Tolerate silence — let the participant think; filling pauses kills reflection.
  • Record with consent — and say what happens to the data before you start (the workshop's own rule).
  • Keep the interviewee talking ~80% of the time — if you're talking more, you're interviewing yourself.

Case study recap · Cortana smart pill dispenser

The interviews were necessary to form a holistic understanding of end users — the flexibility of personal interaction reveals emotions, sensitive issues and the participant's own terminology that self-report can't. Design choices to notice: participants were opted-in (15 residents + 10 staff); recording was audio-only, consented, analysed later; topics were broad with open guiding questions; rapport preceded substance; and the payoff came only after transcription and analysis. Every one of those choices maps onto a lecture point above — that mapping is exactly what the quiz asks.
