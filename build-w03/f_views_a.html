WEEK 3 Roadmap — surveys & interviews

What week 3 covers, why it matters for your assignments, and the two case studies that anchor everything.

Why this week exists

Week 2 taught you to configure a study — questions, methods, participants, budget. Week 3 hands you the two instruments you will actually build for your assignments: the questionnaire/survey (Part A) and the research interview (Part B). Both look easy and both are full of traps: one badly worded question can invalidate a whole dataset, and one leading probe can bias an entire interview. The lectures, the two case studies and both workshop sheets all drill the same underlying skill — getting wording right.

Case study 1 · Daniela's COVID survey (Part A)

Daniela, a data scientist at an IT consultancy, is commissioned by the government to design a digital platform for public-health communications about COVID-19. There is no existing data on whether people changed behaviour in response to communications, and the pandemic is moving fast — so the team runs a large-scale survey through Facebook (Facebook Insights to reach people across states/territories; Ads Manager campaigns to recruit respondents). Because Facebook respondents may not represent the wider population, the survey opens with a demographics section (age, sex, education). They fix 4 topics: (1) underlying medical conditions, (2) adoption of preventive behaviours, (3) perception of the public-health system's response, (4) awareness of current public-health advice. Design decisions: keep it under 10 minutes; prioritise closed multiple-choice over open-ended; use 5-point Likert scales for confidence ratings (1 = not confident at all … 5 = very confident); offer WHO-based options for preventive behaviours (face mask, hand sanitiser, more frequent hand washing, increased social distancing) plus “Other”; and pilot internally with colleagues and friends first to check display, meaningfulness and timing before the public launch.

Why it matters: this case is the template for Workshop 3A and for your own survey work — topics → question forms → scales → categories → pilot → launch.

Case study 2 · Cortana smart pill dispenser (Part B)

Microsoft's team improving Cortana gets ethics approval to research a smart pill dispenser for a nursing home. Observations show most senior residents rely on staff for medication; understaffing means medication is often late. The dispenser would broadcast a voice reminder at the right time — but mobility is still an issue, so the team interviews residents and staff. Sarah (UX designer) interviews 15 residents (opted in, aged 60–80, 8 male / 7 female, varied chronic and temporary conditions and mobility levels) and 10 staff, face-to-face at the nursing home, audio-recorded. Broad topics: daily routines; daily challenges with medication; experience with smart-home assistants; degrees of mobility. Guiding questions stay open; she builds rapport first (Vera is asked about her favourite board games). Key line: the audio only becomes insight after transcription and analysis.

Why it matters: this is the anatomy of a real interview study — and it reappears in the quiz as detail questions (numbers, roles, why interviews were chosen).

WEEK 3 · PART A Surveys & questionnaires

Everything from the Part A slides, the SurveyMonkey reading and the Daniela case — the concepts the quiz tests.

Survey vs questionnaire — the difference that keeps coming up

SurveyQuestionnaire
The broader term — it describes the content, the method and the analysis. The whole research process: designing and distributing the questions, collecting, aggregating and analysing responses.A written set of questions — just the instrument, the form itself. Every survey contains a questionnaire, but a questionnaire can exist alone (e.g. a signup form).
Includes: individual questions ✓ · delivery of the questionnaire ✓ · analysis of responses ✓Includes: individual questions ✓ · delivery ✗ · analysis ✗
Exam-ready line: a questionnaire is the set of written questions; a survey is that set plus the entire process around it (design → distribution → collection → aggregation → analysis). The lecture cites surveymonkey.com/mp/survey-vs-questionnaire.

What information a survey can obtain

The slides list six kinds of information: facts & knowledge (biography) opinions / beliefs attitudes motives actions (past behaviour) likely future behaviour. Notice the design consequence: each kind wants a different question form — you ask actions with frequency scales, attitudes with Likert scales, and you only get at motives well with open or carefully framed questions.

When is a questionnaire appropriate?

All four conditions from the slides:
  1. We have a good idea of what to expect (the space is mapped enough to pre-write options).
  2. We have (a) literate respondents, or (b) time to administer it to them (otherwise they can't self-complete).
  3. We are interested in counts, frequencies — quantitative patterns across many people.
  4. We want to generalise to the larger population.

If instead you need to explore the unknown, dig into why, or handle sensitive/emotional territory, that is when interviews (Part B) take over.

Types of survey study — cross-sectional vs longitudinal

DesignWhat it isTrade-off
Cross-sectionalOne questionnaire, one point in time — a snapshot of the population. Daniela's COVID survey is the model case.Cheap, fast; but can't show change over time or cause–effect ordering.
LongitudinalThe same people re-surveyed repeatedly (a panel) or different samples drawn from the same population over time (trend study).Shows change and can establish what came first; expensive, slow, suffers panel attrition (people drop out).
Quiz trap: “one snapshot of many people” = cross-sectional; “same people followed across waves” = longitudinal (panel). A cohort surveyed once a year for four years is longitudinal, not four cross-sectionals.

Deployment — the tools a survey can ride on

The options (the learning outcome explicitly asks for these):
  • Online survey platforms — Qualtrics (Monash's own platform; the workshop's mock survey runs on it), SurveyMonkey, Google Forms: build, distribute by link, auto-aggregate. Best for literate self-completing respondents at scale.
  • Social-media recruitment + platform — Daniela's pattern: Facebook Insights to scope the audience, Ads Manager campaigns to recruit, survey link to capture. Reach is huge; representativeness is not — hence the demographics section.
  • Email / mailing lists — direct links to known populations (students, customers); watch self-selection bias in who clicks.
  • Face-to-face or phone administration — a trained person reads the questionnaire aloud and records answers; the slide's condition (b) “time to administer (if not literate)”. Expensive per response, but reaches anyone.
  • Paper / intercept — clinics, street intercepts, lecture-theatre handouts; works offline; data entry afterwards is the cost.

Rule of thumb: self-completion online when you have literate respondents and a good frame; administered (human-read) when you don't.

Sampling — how respondents get chosen

The slides name two approaches; the quiz (and every methods unit) expects the full menu. Both families below are fair game:

ApproachMechanismWatch out for
Simple random samplingEvery member of the population has an equal chance of selection (drawn from a complete list).Needs a sampling frame (list); slow to build big n.
Systematic samplingOrder the frame, pick a random start, then take every k-th member (e.g. every 25th student).Dangerous if the list has a hidden pattern with the same period as k (e.g. every 25th happens to be an international student).
Stratified samplingSplit the population into subgroups (strata) that matter (faculty, age, sex), then random-sample within every stratum — usually in proportion to size.Needs to know each member's stratum in advance; guarantees representation, not automatic generalisability.
Cluster samplingSplit the population into natural groups (clusters) — tutorial classes, suburbs — randomly pick whole clusters, survey everyone inside them.Cheaper to run (fewer sites); less precise because people in a cluster resemble each other.
ApproachMechanismWatch out for
Convenience samplingWhoever is easiest to reach — your class, your feed, passers-by.Cheap and instant; almost never representative. Daniela's Facebook ads were effectively this.
Snowball samplingExisting respondents identify more respondents — recruitment chains through networks.Biased toward connected/visible members; the go-to method for hidden or hard-to-reach groups.
Purposive (judgemental) samplingResearcher hand-picks participants believed to be informative — experts, key informants, extreme cases.Depends entirely on the researcher's judgement; no statistical generalisation.
Quota samplingFix target numbers per subgroup (e.g. 50 domestic + 50 international) and fill the quotas with whoever is available.Looks like stratified but is non-probability — the filling is convenience, so the sample can still skew within quotas.
Voluntary / self-selection samplingPeople choose themselves in — link posts, “click here to give your opinion”.The SETU pattern: the keen and the annoyed over-answer; silent majority invisible.
How to choose: do you have a frame (a list of the population)? → probability family (random / systematic / stratified / cluster — stratify when subgroup balance matters, cluster when travel is the cost). No frame, or a hidden group? → non-probability family (convenience is fastest, snowball reaches the hidden, purposive targets expertise, quota forces rough balance, voluntary is cheapest and most biased). Whichever you use: collect demographics and report the bias — that is Daniela's lesson.

Sources cited on the slide: statpac.com/surveys/sampling.htm; the extended family is standard research-methods taxonomy your quiz draws on. Daniela's team effectively used a convenience/volunteer channel (Facebook ads) — which is exactly why they front-loaded a demographics section: when the channel isn't representative, you measure the skew so you can read results by segment.

Response-rate physics (SurveyMonkey reading — real numbers)

  • SurveyMonkey deploys 3.5 million+ surveys a year.
  • Questionnaires with 40 questions have ~10% lower response rates than 10-question ones.
  • People spend ~10 minutes on a questionnaire regardless of its length — so extra questions don't get extra time, they get less attention per question.
  • Therefore: fewer, sharper questions = more thought per answer. This is why Daniela's team capped the survey at 10 minutes and prioritised closed questions.

Question types — how the whole taxonomy fits together

First split: what is the question measuring?
ClassCapturesTypical forms
Factual / demographicWho the respondent is and what is true of them (age band, faculty, enrolment status)Banded ranges, single-choice categories
BehaviouralWhat they actually do / did (actions, past behaviour, likely future behaviour)Frequency scales, numeric bands, checklists
AttitudinalWhat they think and feel (opinions, beliefs, attitudes, motives)Likert, semantic differential, ranking, open-ended
Second split: how is it answered?

Closed — fixed options the respondent picks from (dichotomous, multiple-choice, multi-select, Likert, semantic differential, ranking, banded numeric, matrix). Fast to answer and analyse; requires you to already know the answer space. Open — free text. Slow and expensive to analyse; reaches motives and the unexpected. Daniela's closed-first rule exists because her team needed timely counts — open questions were rationed, not banned.

Scale levels to name-drop: nominal categories with no order (faculty, ethnicity) · ordinal ordered categories without equal gaps (Never → Very often; Likert points) · interval/ratio numbers where differences are meaningful (hours, kg, WAM). The level decides what statistics are legal later — you can average hours (ratio) but not faculty codes (nominal).

Question forms & response scales — the full design palette

FormUse it forRules
Dichotomous (Yes/No, True/False)Simple facts, screening questionsDon't use for attitudes — collapses nuance; follow with a probe question if needed
Closed multiple-choice (one answer)Counts & frequencies; fast analysisOptions must be exhaustive (incl. “Other”) and mutually exclusive (no overlap)
Multi-select (choose all that apply)Behaviours taken, sources usedMake clear multiple answers are allowed
Likert scale (5-point typical)Attitudes, agreement, confidence, satisfactionBalanced anchors (1 = not confident at all … 5 = very confident), odd point count allows a neutral middle
Semantic differentialConnotation/attitude between two bipolar adjectives — “Zoom classes were: Impossible to use ····· Extremely easy to use” (example survey Q8)Two opposite anchored poles with numbered steps between; treat each step as ordinal
Frequency scaleHow often a behaviour happensLabels in logical order (Never → Rarely → Sometimes → Frequently); or exact counts per week/month
Banded ranges (e.g. age, income)Sensitive numeric dataBands must not overlap; always offer “Prefer not to say”
RankingPriorities (“top 3 of these 8”)Keep the list short (≤8) or rankings get noisy
Matrix / gridSame scale applied to many items at once (example survey Q14)State the time frame for every row; keep rows few; watch rendering bugs
Open-endedMotives, the unexpected, “why”Expensive to analyse — use sparingly; one clear ask per question

The ten classic wording pitfalls (the logic behind Workshop 3A Activity 1)

Every flawed question in the workshop sheet is an instance of one of these patterns. Learn the pattern, not the example — the quiz swaps in new examples:

PitfallHow it breaks your dataFix pattern
1 · Leading / loaded wordingTelegraphs the “right” answer; measures agreement with you, not the respondent's view.Strip value words & prestige appeals; state both sides neutrally.
2 · Double-barrelledTwo questions fused; a single answer can't represent both → uninterpretable.Split into one ask per question.
3 · Double negative“Agree that students should not…” flips logic; respondents misread.Affirmative stem + balanced agree–disagree scale.
4 · Vague / undefined terms“Young people”, “these days”, “often” mean different things to different respondents.Define population, timeframe, unit.
5 · Presumptuous / screening failureAssumes facts (a son, a theft) not established; alienates respondents.Screen question first; neutral conditional phrasing.
6 · Unanswerable (respondent can't know)Asks for estimates of others' behaviour or unknowable totals → garbage numbers.Ask about the respondent's own behaviour in a bounded period.
7 · Sensitive without safeguardsIncome, ethnicity, cheating → refusal or lying; non-response bias.Banded ranges, “Prefer not to say”, explain why it's asked.
8 · Overlapping / non-exhaustive optionsTwo bands both fit (or none fit) → forced wrong answers.Mutually exclusive + collectively exhaustive + “Other”.
9 · Unbalanced scale / unordered labelsSkewed midpoints or scrambled frequency labels distort distributions.Symmetric anchors in logical order.
10 · Scope too big“What can be done about global warming?” → unanalysable essays.Narrow to one aspect with structured options.
