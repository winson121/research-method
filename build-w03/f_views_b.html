WEEK 3 · PART A Workshop 3A · solved — Surveys & Questionnaires

The studio sheet has 3 activities: question critique, developing a questionnaire, and peer feedback. Below: the reusable template for each, then multiple complete model answers with the reasoning made explicit, so you can speak to any of them in class.

Activity 1 · Question critique (Parts A & B)

The template — critique any survey question in 4 moves
  1. Name the flaw(s) — map the wording onto the pitfall taxonomy from the Surveys section (leading, double-barrelled, double negative, vague terms, presumptuous, unanswerable, sensitive-without-safeguards, overlapping options, unbalanced scale, oversized scope).
  2. Show how it breaks the data — what would respondents actually do (guess, lie, abandon, answer a different question) and what that does to your analysis.
  3. State the rewrite rule — the general pattern that fixes this class of flaw.
  4. Write the improved question — one concrete rewrite, with its response options.

Part C is presenting this back to another group: group your findings by pitfall, lead with the two worst questions, and give the one-line fix for each. (The sheet also asks you to complete a mock Qualtrics survey in 5–10 minutes — that's you experiencing a real instrument from the respondent's side; notice which questions felt awkward and why.)

How the pattern logic applies here: every model answer below follows the same 4 moves. The diagnosis references pitfall numbers from the taxonomy table; the rewrite applies that pitfall's fix pattern. If you can reproduce this move sequence for a brand-new flawed question, you can handle any critique the quiz or the class throws at you.

Q1 · “What is wrong with young people these days and what can we do about it?”

Flaws: double-barrelled (two questions: what's wrong + what to do); loaded negative presupposition (“what is wrong” assumes something is); vague terms (“young people”, “these days”); oversized scope (pitfalls 1, 2, 4, 10).

Why it breaks the data: respondents who disagree that anything is wrong have no honest answer; those who answer are answering different questions; “young people” could mean 15-year-olds or 35-year-olds, so answers aren't comparable.

Rewrite rule: split the asks, define the population with an age band and the period with a timeframe, remove the presupposition.

Improved version A (opinion, defined terms): “Thinking about people aged 18–25 today, how much do you agree or disagree with each of the following? — They have good job prospects / They show respect to others / They are optimistic about the future. (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (behaviour, defined terms): “In the past 12 months, how often have people under 25 in your local area done anything that you found disruptive? (Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Frequently)” — notice the loaded “what is wrong” becomes a neutral, observable behaviour with a bounded timeframe.

Q2 · “Do you go swimming? [1] Never, [2] Rarely, [3] Frequently, [4] Sometimes”

Flaws: unordered frequency labels (Sometimes listed after Frequently — scales must run low→high); vague frequency words with no anchors; stem/scale mismatch (a yes/no stem offered a frequency scale) (pitfalls 4, 8, 9).

Why it breaks the data: category order scrambles the underlying continuum, so the data can't be ranked or even consistently coded; “frequently” means weekly to one respondent and daily to another.

Rewrite rule: put response categories in logical order, anchor the vague ends, and make the stem match the scale.

Improved version A (ordered labels): “How often do you go swimming? (Never / Less than once a month / 1–3 times a month / At least once a week)”

Improved version B (numeric bands): “In a typical month, how many times do you go swimming? (0 / 1–2 / 3–5 / 6 or more)” — numeric bands remove all ambiguity about what “frequently” means.

Q3 · “What do you think can be done about global warming?”

Flaws: oversized unbounded open question; “what do you think” invites essays; answers will be incomparable and unanalysable at any sample size (pitfalls 4, 10).

Why it breaks the data: you'd get 200 unique essays you can't aggregate; a survey exists to produce counts and frequencies, so the question defeats the instrument.

Rewrite rule: narrow to one aspect and give a structured response; if depth is needed, use one bounded open question or switch method to interviews.

Improved version A (forced priority): “Which ONE of these would do most to reduce Australia's emissions? (More renewable energy / Better public transport / Stricter building standards / Reducing meat consumption / None of these / Don't know)”

Improved version B (own behaviour): “Which of these have you done in the past 12 months to reduce your environmental impact? Select all that apply. (Reduced car use / Reduced meat or dairy / Reduced flying / Switched to renewable energy / None)”

Q4 · “Most medical professionals think that smoking causes lung cancer; do you: Strongly agree … Strongly disagree”

Flaws: leading via authority/bandwagon appeal — the preamble pressures agreement and turns the item into a test of whether you'll side with doctors (pitfall 1).

Why it breaks the data: social desirability and prestige cues inflate agreement; you no longer measure the respondent's belief, you measure their reluctance to contradict experts.

Rewrite rule: delete every prestige preamble; ask the respondent's own view with a balanced stem.

Improved version A (belief, balanced): “To what extent do you agree or disagree: Smoking causes lung cancer. (Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither / Agree / Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (evidence strength): “In your view, how strong is the evidence that smoking causes lung cancer? (No evidence / Weak / Moderate / Strong / Very strong)” — this separates belief from perceived evidence, which is usually what the researcher actually wants.

Q5 · “Do you agree that students should not have to take an exam at the end of their course? [a] Yes; [b] No.”

Flaws: double negative (agree + “should not”) — cognitive load flips the logic; acquiescence bias from the “Do you agree” stem; yes/no collapses all nuance (pitfalls 1, 3, 9).

Why it breaks the data: many respondents will answer the opposite of what they mean; Yes/No also hides strength of feeling, so a 51–49 split tells you nothing about intensity.

Rewrite rule: make the statement affirmative, use a balanced agree–disagree scale, never ask “do you agree”.

Improved version A (Likert): “Students should have to take an exam at the end of their course. (Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither / Agree / Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (forced choice between positions): “Which statement comes closer to your view? A: Every student should sit a final exam. B: Students should be able to complete a course without a final exam. (A / B / Neither)”

Q6 · “Has your son ever stolen anything? If so when?”

Flaws: presumptuous screening failure (assumes you have a son); sensitive and accusatory (implying theft by your child); compound conditional demanding a precise date for a stigmatised act; no refusal option (pitfalls 5, 7).

Why it breaks the data: childless respondents can't answer; parents of daughters are excluded; those who can answer face a threat — expect refusals, lies or abandoned surveys (non-response bias exactly on the sensitive item).

Rewrite rule: screen first, neutralise the language, bound the timeframe, always offer “Prefer not to say”.

Improved version A (screen + neutral + bounded): “Do you have any children? (No / Yes — son(s) only / Yes — daughter(s) only / Yes — both)” → If yes: “Thinking about the past 12 months, has any of your children taken something from a shop or person without permission? (Yes / No / Prefer not to say)”

Improved version B (non-accusatory framing): “Has your child ever been spoken to at school or by another family about taking things that weren't theirs? (Yes / No / Not applicable / Prefer not to say)”

Q7 · “What do you think about the left-wing media's attempt to blackmail the Government?”

Flaws: heavily loaded political framing (“left-wing”, “blackmail”); asserts unverified facts as premise; merges actor + action + evaluation into one ask (pitfalls 1, 2, 4).

Why it breaks the data: respondents who reject the premise can't answer honestly; the item measures agreement with the framing, not media perceptions; politically, half your sample will shut down.

Rewrite rule: replace value-laden nouns with neutral referents, split actor from action from evaluation, never embed disputed facts in the stem.

Improved version A (trust battery): “How much do you trust each of the following to report political news accurately? (TV broadcasters / Newspapers / Online-only outlets / Social media) (No trust / Little / Moderate / High)”

Improved version B (neutral perception): “How much do you agree or disagree: Recent coverage of the Government by Australian media has been fair. (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree)” — one claim, neutral wording, balanced scale.

Q8 · “How much food do you think the average family throws away in a week?”

Flaws: unanswerable — the respondent cannot know what the “average family” discards; unit undefined (“how much” = kg? litres? dollars?); invites a wild guess (pitfalls 4, 6, 8).

Why it breaks the data: you'd collect confident-sounding fiction; two respondents with identical households give different answers because they guessed in different units.

Rewrite rule: ask about the respondent's own behaviour in a bounded period, define the unit, offer a “Not sure”.

Improved version A (own household, unit given): “Roughly how much food does YOUR household throw away in a typical week? (None / Less than 1 kg / 1–2 kg / 3–5 kg / More than 5 kg / Not sure)”

Improved version B (behaviour proxy): “In the past week, how often did your household throw away food that was left over or had gone off? (Never / Once / 2–3 times / 4+ times)” — frequency of disposal is estimable; kilograms of “average families” are not.

Q9 · “How much do you earn?”

Flaws: sensitive item with no safeguards: exact figure demanded, no bands, no refusal option (pitfall 7).

Why it breaks the data: expect refusals, rounding chaos and survey abandonment right at the item — precisely the people who refuse are often those you most need, biasing every cross-tab that uses income.

Rewrite rule: band it, make it skippable, and mark it optional unless essential.

Improved version A (bands + refusal): “Which of the following best describes your annual income before tax? (Under $20,000 / $20,000–$39,999 / $40,000–$59,999 / $60,000–$99,999 / $100,000 or more / Prefer not to say)”

Improved version B (optional, broad bands): “Roughly where does your yearly income fall? (Below average / About average / Above average / Prefer not to say)” — coarse but honest when the precise bands would scare respondents away.

Q10 · “What is your ethnicity?”

Flaws: sensitive with no category list, no self-describe escape, no refusal option; free-text ethnicity is nearly impossible to aggregate consistently (pitfalls 4, 7, 8).

Why it breaks the data: respondents write “Australian”, “Chinese-Malaysian”, “mixed” — each defensible, none comparable; some will abandon rather than answer.

Rewrite rule: use the standard classification of the study country (for Australia, the ABS standard), add “Other (please specify)” and “Prefer not to say”, and state why the data is collected.

Improved version A (single choice, standard list): “Which of the following best describes your ethnic background? (Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander / North-West European / Southern and Eastern European / North African and Middle Eastern / South-East Asian / North-East Asian / Southern and Central Asian / Peoples of the Americas / Sub-Saharan African / Pacific Islander / Other (please specify) / Prefer not to say)”

Improved version B (multi-select heritage): “Do you identify with any of these cultural or ethnic groups? Select ALL that apply. [same list] + Prefer not to say” — lets mixed-heritage respondents answer truthfully.

Present-back tip (Part C): don't recite all ten fixes — group them (“three questions were leading, two had undefined terms, two were double-barrelled…”), name the worst offender, give its one-line rewrite. That is what tutors reward.

Activity 2 · Developing the questionnaire (Parts A & B)

The brief (from the sheet): “Going to university is a major life change for many people. We are interested in how people adjust to this new social situation (student life).” You interpret it through your own experience, then: Part A — beyond demographics (nationality, age, course…), identify 2 topics important or interesting to explore. Part B — for each topic, develop 4 questions, and for every question be clear about the justification: why you are asking it (in relation to the topic), and the form of the question (type, wording, response scale).

The template — how to develop topics
  1. Test breadth: can you immediately imagine 4+ distinct questions for it? If not, it's a question, not a topic.
  2. Test importance/interest: does it plausibly drive whether a student adjusts well? Say why in one sentence.
  3. Test distinctness: do your two topics overlap? If answers to topic 2 predict answers to topic 1, merge or re-scope.
  4. Anchor it: one line from your own experience or a rapid literature scan that justifies it.
The template — how to justify each question (the marking grid tutors use)
Justification elementWhat a full-credit answer states
Why askedWhat this item measures and how it feeds the topic (e.g. “size of the support network — because isolation predicts drop-out”)
TypeClosed single / multi-select / Likert / frequency / banded numeric / ranking / open — and why that type fits the information sought
WordingOne clause showing a pitfall was avoided (defined terms, no leading, one ask only, bounded timeframe)
ScaleThe exact options/anchors and why they're ordered, exhaustive, exclusive (or deliberately open)

Model answer set 1 — topics chosen

Topic A: “Balancing study with social life” — how students allocate time and handle clashes. Broad (time use, interference, perceived balance, trade-off rules) and central to adjustment: the classic first-year failure mode is either all-study burnout or all-social slide. Anchored by group experience: every member had lived a clash week.

Topic B: “Building a support network and feeling you belong” — friends made, channels used, loneliness, belonging. Distinct from Topic A (A is time allocation, B is connection), and well-evidenced in the transition literature: belonging predicts persistence.

Demographics block: age band (18–20 / 21–24 / 25–30 / 31+, bands not exact year); enrolment status (domestic / international); faculty; year level; living arrangement (with family / student housing / shared rental / alone). All banded, all with “Prefer not to say”.

Activity 3 · Questionnaire critique & feedback

The template — one feedback note per reviewed question (minimum 2 required by the sheet)
  1. Quote the question exactly as presented (so the group can find it).
  2. Name one strength — what it does right (good scale, clean wording, useful information type). Feedback that is only negative gets ignored.
  3. Name the issue — which pitfall, and what it would do to the data.
  4. Give a concrete rewrite — wording + options, not “rephrase this”.
  5. Ask one designer question — e.g. “What will you compare this against?” — to make them defend the justification.
Worked example — three notes written as if reviewing another group's “adjusting to student life” questionnaire
Reviewed questionFeedback note (strength / issue / rewrite / designer question)
“Don't you agree that the university should provide more social events for new students?”Strength: targets an actionable topic. Issue: leading double-negative-free but still leading — “Don't you agree” demands assent (pitfall 1). Rewrite: “The university provides enough social events for new students. (Strongly disagree → Strongly agree)”. Designer question: would you still know what to fix if 80% answer “agree”?
“How many hours do you study AND sleep per day?”Strength: both quantities matter to adjustment. Issue: double-barrelled — one number can't be both (pitfall 2). Rewrite: split into two banded items. Designer question: which of the two did you plan to correlate with stress?
“What is your age? [open box]”Strength: simple. Issue: exact age is more identifying than needed and yields messy values (pitfall 7-lite). Rewrite: bands 18–20 / 21–24 / 25–30 / 31+ / Prefer not to say. Designer question: what decision needs age at finer granularity than bands?

In the room: presentations run 2–3 minutes per group with 10–15 minutes of critique; then your group spends 10 minutes applying the feedback to improve your own questions — keep the rewrites you accepted and be ready to say which you rejected and why (that defence is itself assessed thinking).
