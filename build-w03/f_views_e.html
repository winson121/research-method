The Part B assessment template, explained

The template itself is minimal — a cover page (“ITI5125 Research Methods in IT · Week 3 (Part B) · Planning and conducting an interview · Assessment Template”) and two placeholders: Task 1. <Interview schedule here> and Task 2. <Interview transcription here>. The marks live in what you put in the placeholders:

TaskWhat assessors look for
Task 1 · Interview scheduleA complete schedule: topic focus, ordered questions covering multiple question types, planned probes under each question, a consent/rapport opening, and a catch-all close. Reuse the Workshop 3B model schedules — they already meet this bar.
Task 2 · Interview transcriptionA faithful transcript of your recorded interview: speaker labels (Interviewer / Participant), timestamps at reasonable intervals, [inaudible] markers where audio is unclear, and — this is the point of the exercise — a transcript you produced yourself, because transcribing your own interview is how you “understand nuances in the data and expose latent topics” (Moodle).
Transcription types (Moodle links to newmediaservices.com.au/types-of-transcription)
TypeWhat it keepsUse it for
VerbatimEvery word, filler (um, uh), false starts, pauses, laughterDiscourse/analysis work where how things are said matters
Intelligent verbatimEvery word minus fillers and stumbles; light tidyingThe default for research interviews — readable, still faithful
Edited / summarySummarised or smoothed into formal proseNot for analysis — only for communication extracts
Model transcript excerpt (intelligent verbatim) — from the Workshop 3B grocery schedule
I: Think back to your last grocery trip or order. Walk me through how it started — before anything was in the trolley.
P: Sure. Last Saturday, I opened the Woolworths app on the couch — we'd already used up most of the week. The list from last time was sitting there, so I basically just topped it up.
I: The list from last time — so the app remembered?
P: Yeah, “previously purchased”. That's… honestly the main reason I use the app. I can't be bothered writing a list.
I: Who wrote the list originally?
P: [laughs] Nobody, that's the thing. It's just everything I've ever bought, in order. My wife edits it on her phone and things appear that I've never bought — she adds them.
I: Anything about that shared list ever cause… friction?
P: Oh, constantly. She'll add things and I won't see it until the delivery arrives and half of it's missing because it was “unavailable” and the substitution was [inaudible].
I: Tell me more about the substitutions.
…(timestamps every 2–5 min in the real transcript; [inaudible] marked, never guessed)…

Why this excerpt is good: speaker labels on every turn · probes pursue the thread (list → who wrote it → friction → substitutions) · [inaudible] instead of invention · the “latent topic” — a household negotiation hidden inside a grocery app — surfaces exactly because the interviewer followed the thread instead of the schedule.

The consent form, clause by clause

The provided consent form (ITI5125 T3 2024 version) has an explanatory statement and a per-item consent table. Each clause below is quoted or paraphrased from the form with the reason it exists:

ClauseWhat it guarantees / why it matters
Explanatory statement: who, for what, and what happens to the dataNames the researcher and states it's for an ITI5125 assessment; data “will not be used for any published research, but a transcribed version will be viewed by the teaching team when marking”. Establishes informed consent and sets expectations.
Chief Investigator named (Prof. Patrick Olivier, the unit's Chief Examiner)Gives the participant a senior, accountable contact independent of the student researcher.
“I consent to take part in the interview” (Yes/No)The core voluntary-participation tick.
“I consent for audio recording” + “transcribing on the interviewer's personal computer”Recording and transcription are separate consents — a participant can agree to the interview but not the recording; and the personal-computer clause is honest about where the file lives (a privacy risk being disclosed).
Secure storage at Monash, accessible only to the teaching team, for the life of the unitConfidentiality + retention limits: data doesn't wander, and it has an expiry.
“My name will not be deliberately shared with the teaching team”De-identification promise — pseudonymisation of the transcript.
Voluntary; withdrawal “up to the time that names are removed and marking has commenced”Right of withdrawal with an honest deadline — once marking starts, withdrawal is impossible; the clause defines that boundary instead of pretending otherwise.
Participant can request access to the recording and transcriptionTransparency: the participant owns their own data enough to inspect it.

Practical checklist before your real interview: fill in your name/email in the explanatory statement · print two copies (one for the participant) · walk the participant through each row before they tick · collect signature + date · store the form with the recording, not in the transcript file.

Dissecting the provided survey example (Part A) — question by question

The example survey (“S1 2024 Week 4 Assessment Survey”, designed by a fictional IT consulting company to understand how technology affects student performance) is itself a critique object. Working through it with the workshop-3A template:

ItemDiagnosis (template move 1–2)
Q1 “completely anonymous” vs Q2 “What is your full name?”Internal contradiction — the instrument claims anonymity then collects direct identifiers. Either drop the name or change the claim; as written it destroys trust and possibly consent validity.
Q7 “hours a day on a device” (open number) vs Q12 “time on device for learning” (bands 0 / 1–3 / 4–10 / 10–14 / 14+)Overlapping bands at the boundary (10 appears in both “4–10” and “10–14”) — pitfall 8; and two near-duplicate device-time questions with different frames (“a device” vs “for learning”) invite inconsistent answers.
Q11 “Why are assignments so hard and how do you manage to complete them?”Loaded double-barrelled — presumes assignments are hard (pitfall 1) and asks two things at once (pitfall 2); the three options are all self-blaming, so disagreement has no voice.
Q14 matrix “How much time do you spend on each activity?” with rows Reading / Writing reports / … and columns Less than 1h / 2–4h / 5–10h / More than 10hMatrix grids save space but hide scale ambiguity — “per day? per week?” is never stated; the survey export even shows the classic rendering failure (the “ooooo” radio-row misalignment). Also bands gap: 1–2 hours missing entirely.
Q16 “Have you ever cheated in an exam or an assignment? Yes / Maybe / No”Sensitive item, no safeguards (pitfall 7): no anonymity honesty, no Prefer-not-to-say, and “Maybe” is a non-answer option that invites noise. Expect social-desirability bias.
What it does well (template move: name strengths too)Q3 (gender) includes non-binary and offers “Prefer not to say”; Q10/Q17 use anchored endpoints (“Extremely bad → Extremely good”); the opening states purpose and estimated time (15 min) — the Daniela pattern. Worth flagging in feedback: Q4 (age) and Q5 (faculty) are banded but have no opt-out.
