WEEK 3 · DEEP DIVE The interviewer's craft — probes, focus groups, sensitive topics

Everything on this page sits one level below the interview-types material: not which interview to run, but how to run it well once the participant is in the chair. These are the standard qualitative-interviewing canon (Spradley 1979; Kvale & Brinkmann), and the quiz draws scenario questions straight from it.

Types of probes

A probe is a short follow-up that invites more of the participant's meaning without adding any of yours. Five standard forms:

ProbeSounds likeWhat it buys you
Elaboration probe“Tell me more about that.” · “What happened next?”Expansion of whatever the participant just said; keeps the thread moving with zero new content from you.
Clarification probe“What do you mean by ‘falls over’?”Pins down the participant's own term so you code it faithfully instead of guessing.
Exemplification probe“Can you give an example?”Grounds an abstract claim in one concrete episode, which is where the analysable detail lives.
Echo probeRepeat their last few words as a question: “The list writes itself?”Signals listening and hands the turn back; asserts nothing at all.
Summary probe“So would it be fair to say…?”Checks your interpretation before moving on, and gives the participant a chance to correct you. The safety net against mis-hearing.
The rule that ties all five together: probes elicit detail without steering. The moment your follow-up asserts an interpretation, demands agreement or embeds an expected answer, it stops being a probe and becomes a leading question. This distinction is the single most quizzed point in this section.

Focus groups

The week-3 configurations table lists the focus group as the group-based interview configuration (alongside one-to-one, debate-based and multiple-interviewer setups). One moderator, a small recruited group, one semi-structured guide.

Why the interaction is the data: eight individual interviews give you eight separate accounts of what people say they think. A focus group lets you watch participants challenge, agree, joke, defer and persuade each other in real time, so group norms, consensus-seeking and social influence surface live rather than being reported second-hand. You are studying the conversation, not just harvesting eight shorter interviews at once.

The moderator's role is to facilitate, not dominate: set ground rules up front, keep dominant voices in check, and actively draw out quiet members (a direct invitation or round-robin turn is the standard move). The moderator's own opinions stay out of the room, because one expressed view reframes every answer that follows.

Practical norms: typically 6–10 participants, 60–90 minutes, and a semi-structured guide so the session stays comparable across groups while leaving room to chase what the group raises.

Choosing the configuration
Choose…When…
SurveyYou have a good idea of what to expect and want counts, frequencies and generalisation to a population.
One-to-one interviewYou need depth on individual experience, sensitive territory, or the participant's own terminology; privacy matters.
Focus groupThe research question is about shared norms, consensus or how people influence each other; the exchange between participants is itself the evidence.

Sensitive topics

Income, health, medication dependence, cheating: sensitive questions can be asked, but only with deliberate technique:

  • Place them late in the schedule, after easy warm-up and substantive questions have built the rapport you will spend here.
  • Use permissive framing that normalises both answers: “Some people find bills pile up, others don't. What fits your situation?”
  • State the right to skip explicitly, out loud, before the sensitive block.
  • Never press. A pause or a look away is a skip signal. Offer a graceful exit and move on.
  • React neutrally to whatever comes back: no wincing, no praise, no surprise. Your reaction is data about what is acceptable to say.
The slides connect this to interview type: unstructured interviews suit sensitive topics, because with no rigid schedule there is nothing forcing the question at a fixed moment. The conversation can approach, retreat and return when the participant is ready.

Common interviewer errors

ErrorWhy it corrupts the dataCorrective habit
LeadingMeasures compliance with your expectation, not the participant's view.Neutral stems; put your hypotheses in the analysis, not the question.
Double-barrelled spoken questionsTwo questions fused into one; a single answer cannot represent both halves.Write the schedule down and ask one thing at a time.
InterruptingTrains the participant to give short, safe answers and cuts off the interesting half of sentences.Take notes on what to probe and wait for the turn to end.
Filling silencesThe best material often arrives after a pause; your filler replaces it.Count to five silently before speaking.
Over-summarisingPuts your words in their mouth; participants agree with summaries they don't hold.Use a summary probe (“so would it be fair to say…”) and invite correction.
Judgmental reactionsSignals which answers are acceptable, so later answers self-censor.Neutral face, neutral words, thank them the same way every time.
Yes/no chainingA run of closed questions converts an interview into a form-filling exercise and forfeit nuance.Open first, closed only to pin a fact down.
Schedule rigidity vs total driftRigid adherence misses what the participant is telling you; total drift loses comparability across interviews.Follow the thread, note where you left the schedule, then return to it. The Cortana model: chase the latent topic, then steer back.

Remote / online interviews

Pros
  • Lower cost: no travel, no venue, easier scheduling.
  • Recording built into the platform (with consent).
  • Wider reach: participants anywhere, including mobility-limited groups such as the Cortana residents.
Cons
  • Lost non-verbal channel, which the slides list as a face-to-face advantage; rapport is harder.
  • Connectivity failures and audio dropout corrupt the recording.
  • Home environments bring privacy limits on sensitive topics.
Mitigations: run a quick tech check before the session, agree a backup phone number or re-booking rule, ask for camera-on where privacy allows, send the consent form digitally in advance, and keep probes extra explicit since the nod-and-smile channel is gone.
