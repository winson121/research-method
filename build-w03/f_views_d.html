WEEK 3 · PART B Workshop 3B · solved — Research Interviews

The sheet has 4 activities: interview topics, interview schedule, conducting the interview, reflections. Template first, then multiple complete model answers with the logic made explicit.

Activity 1 · Interview topics

The brief (from the sheet): plan an interview on “Grocery Shopping and Technology” — how has technology transformed the way people do grocery shopping? The focus must be narrowed through group discussion: consider families / travel / retail / work as lenses, and decide whether you talk about a single specific country or all countries. Deliverable: 5 mini-topics, each with a name, a brief description, and why you plan to ask about it. The template — from giant focus to 5 mini-topics
  1. Pick the lens (one line, group agreement): population + context — e.g. “Australian households with children, everyday weekly shopping, last 2 years”. This is the narrowing the sheet demands.
  2. Brainstorm the whole space (10 minutes, no judging): planning, in-store, online/delivery, payment, budgeting, loyalty, trust, family roles…
  3. Cluster into 4–6 candidate mini-topics — each must be a theme you could ask 4+ questions about, and together they should cover the lens without overlap.
  4. Name, describe, justify each: name = 2–4 words; description = what it covers; why = what it reveals about “how technology transformed grocery shopping”.
How the pattern logic applies: “how has technology transformed grocery shopping?” is unanswerable as one question — it's an area of concern, like week 3A's “adjusting to student life”. The lens converts it into a bounded population and timeframe; the mini-topics partition the space so that each interview question has an obvious home.

Model answer set 1 — lens: Australian family households, weekly shop

Mini-topicDescriptionWhy ask about it
1 · Planning & listsHow the weekly shop is planned: paper lists, phone notes, fridge cameras, reorder apps, shared family lists.Technology enters the shop before the store — planning apps restructure what gets bought; reveals the earliest touchpoint of transformation.
2 · In-store technologySelf-checkout, scan-and-go, shelf labels, store apps with maps/offers, smart trolleys.The most visible transformation — changes the shopper's role (customer becomes co-worker); rich ground for friction stories.
3 · Online shopping & deliverySupermarket websites/apps, click-and-collect, third-party delivery (Uber Eats/Milkrun-style), subscriptions.The biggest structural shift — the shop moves home; contrasts old vs new habits directly.
4 · Budgeting & price toolsPrice comparison, loyalty apps, digital catalogues, cashback, budget-tracking.Economic motive: does technology save money or just collect data? Connects transformation to household finances.
5 · Family roles & decisionsWho does the shop now; how tech changed who decides, who pays, who substitutes items; shared accounts and notifications.Transformation is social, not just technical — roles and negotiations change when the shop is an app on a shared phone.

Model answer set 2 — lens: one country (Australia), the in-store journey

Mini-topicDescriptionWhy ask
1 · The in-store journeyArrival → aisles → shelf → checkout: what tech touches each stage.A grand-tour spine for the whole interview — the participant can narrate it end to end.
2 · Checkout & payment techSelf-checkout, tap-and-go, mobile wallets, receipt apps.The stage with the most reported friction — speed vs control trade-offs.
3 · Loyalty & personalisationFlybuys/Woolworths Rewards-style programs, targeted offers, “you bought this before” prompts.Data-for-discount exchange — tests whether shoppers feel served or surveilled.
4 · Substitution & out-of-stocksApp-chosen replacements, “allow substitutions” settings, refund flows.Where online shopping most visibly changes the product itself.
5 · Trust, privacy & dataWhat data shoppers share knowingly, what they assume is collected, what would be a step too far.The ethical edge of the transformation — saves the most sensitive content for late in the interview (ordering logic).

Model answer set 3 — lens: cross-generational comparison, all channels

1 · Then vs now (how grocery shopping worked 10–15 years ago for this person) · 2 · First adoption (which technology they adopted first and why) · 3 · Daily tools (the apps/devices used every week) · 4 · What got harder (skills, effort, failures) · 5 · What's next (automation they'd accept: smart fridges, auto-replenishment, drones). Justification: this set is optimised for the transformation over time reading of the focus — each topic is one chapter of the same story (past → adoption → present → friction → future), which makes it ideal if your group's interest is change narratives rather than channels.

Country decision (the sheet's own prompt): Set 1 and 2 narrow to Australia — respondents can name real supermarkets, apps and loyalty programs, which produces concrete data; Set 3 stays country-agnostic, trading concreteness for transferability. Either is defensible if you state it; what loses marks is not deciding.

Activity 2 · Interview schedule (10 minutes)

The template — building the schedule
  1. Select 2 mini-topics from Activity 1 (the sheet says 2 — choose ones that flow into each other, e.g. planning → online shopping).
  2. Write 5–6 questions per topic using a variety of question types: at least one opening/rapport, one grand tour, structural follow-ups, one contrast, and probes under every question (“include potential probes for each question, to elicit a more detailed response”).
  3. Order by the funnel: rapport → broad → specific → (sensitive late) → catch-all close.
  4. Time-box to 10 minutes: ~1 min intro + rapport, ~4 min per topic, ~1 min closing. If a topic has 6 questions, that's ~40 seconds per answer — so questions must be answerable in a sentence or two, with probes as the stretch layer.
  5. Record justifications: for each question (why + type), for each probe (what detail it elicits), and for the order (why this sequence).

Model schedule 1 — mini-topics: Planning & lists + Online shopping & delivery

#QuestionTypePotential probesJustification
0(Before recording) “This is a practice interview for ITI5125. It takes about 10 minutes, I'll audio-record it for the class exercise only, and you can pause or stop anytime — is that okay?”Consent preamble—The sheet requires the interviewer to state what the data is for and what happens to it before starting.
1“To start us off — how do you usually do your grocery shopping these days? Just paint me the picture.”Opening / grand tour“How often?” · “Who with?” · “Online, in-store, or both?”Broad, neutral, puts the participant's own routine (not tech jargon) at the centre; doubles as rapport because it's easy and non-sensitive.
2“Think back to your last grocery trip or order. Walk me through how it started — before anything was in the trolley or the cart.”Grand tour (topic 1: planning)“Was there a list?” · “Where does the list live — paper, phone app, memory?” · “Who wrote it?”Forces the specific, recent episode recall that produces concrete data; the probes locate the planning artefacts (mini-topic 1) without naming them first.
3“How do you decide what goes on the list?”Structural“Meal plans? Specials? Habits?” · “Does an app suggest things?”Gets at agency vs automation — is the list theirs or the algorithm's?
4“What role does your phone play in all this?”Structural / tech-focus“Which apps?” · “Notifications or reminders?” · “Shared list with family?”Names the technology explicitly only after the participant described the practice — avoids priming them to perform “tech user”.
5“Tell me about the last time you ordered groceries online or for delivery.”Grand tour (topic 2)“Which platform?” · “What prompted it instead of going in?”Transition to topic 2 via a concrete episode; “last time” bounds recall.
6“What's better about doing it that way — and what's worse?”Contrast“Miss choosing your own fruit?” · “Time saved?” · “Substitutions?”Contrast questions surface values efficiently: the good/bad split maps the transformation's win/loss ledger.
7“Have you ever had a problem with an online order? What happened?”Critical incident“How was it resolved?” · “Did it change how you shop since?”Incidents carry emotion and detail; the behavioural-change probe connects the story back to the research focus.
8“Is there anything about grocery shopping and technology that I haven't asked, that you think I should know?”Closing / catch-all“Anything that surprised you?”The sheet explicitly wants questions that invite unanticipated aspects; this is the safety net that catches what your schedule missed.

Order justification: Q1–Q2 establish the participant's own frame (their words, their routine) before any tech vocabulary appears (Q4) — this ordering avoids priming and follows rapport→broad→specific. Sensitive content (problems, Q7) sits late, after trust is built. The catch-all (Q8) is always last. Timing: intro 1 min · Q1–Q4 ≈ 4 min · Q5–Q7 ≈ 4 min · Q8 + thanks ≈ 1 min. If running over, drop Q3 (its content is partly covered by Q2's probes).

Model schedule 2 — mini-topics: In-store technology + Trust, privacy & data

#QuestionTypePotential probesJustification
1“When you think about your usual supermarket visit, what do you actually do from the moment you walk in?”Grand tour“Which entrance/section first?” · “List in hand or phone?”Lets the participant narrate the journey; tech will surface in the narration where it actually matters to them.
2“Which machines or apps do you use in the store, if any?”Structural + enumeration“Self-checkout? Scan-and-go? Store app?” · “Every time or sometimes?”Enumerates the in-store tech inventory per participant — gives comparable data across interviews.
3“Tell me about the last time self-checkout (or scan-and-go) annoyed you — or delighted you.”Critical incident / contrast“What exactly happened?” · “What did you do next?”“Annoyed or delighted” invites both valences without assuming only frustration; the next-action probe reveals coping.
4“Do you use the store's loyalty program?” (if yes) “What does it give you — and what do you think it takes?”Structural + contrast“Read the terms?” · “Ever changed what you buy because of offers?”“Give vs take” frames the data-for-discount exchange neutrally; second probe connects personalisation to actual behaviour change.
5“How do you feel about the supermarket knowing what you buy?”Attitude (topic 2 opening)“Knows what exactly, do you think?” · “Comfortable or creepy?”Opens the trust topic at the feeling level first — the follow-up separates the emotion from the (often wrong) factual belief about what's collected.
6“Is there any shopping technology you would NOT be willing to use? What crosses the line for you?”Boundary / hypothetical“Smart fridge that reorders? Face-scan checkout? Microphone in the aisle?”The line-drawing question is the purest trust measure — people define privacy by their refusals; probes offer concrete futures so the answer isn't abstract.
7“Anything else about shopping, technology and trust on your mind?”Closing / catch-all—Catch-all; keeps the sensitive topic open-ended at the end so the participant closes on their own terms.

Order justification: the journey is narrated before attitudes are probed (experience → opinion), and the sensitive trust topic is placed after the participant has spent ~5 minutes successfully describing their own competence — late placement maximises candour. Timing: Q1–Q4 ≈ 5 min · Q5–Q7 ≈ 4 min · buffer 1 min.

Activity 3 · Conducting the interview (role-play in threes)

Roles: interviewer · interviewee · observer (the observer exists only in this teaching exercise; real research interviews have just two people — the sheet says so). Run it twice, rotating roles, using the schedule from Activity 2. The interviewer's 60-second pre-interview script (memorise this pattern)
  1. Purpose: “This interview is about grocery shopping and technology, for an ITI5125 workshop exercise.”
  2. Data use: “I'm audio-recording it; the recording and a transcription will be seen only by our group and the tutor, and deleted/kept per the unit's rules.”
  3. Rights: “It's voluntary, you can skip any question, and you can stop at any time.”
  4. Check: “Any questions before we start? Okay if I record?” → wait for a verbal yes.

The sheet is explicit: even without a formal information sheet or consent form (because it's a teaching exercise), the interviewer must be clear about what the data is for and what happens to it. In the real assignment, this becomes the consent form + explanatory statement (see Assignment prep).

The observer's checklist (what to write down — one line per item)
Watch forTick if the interviewer…
Probes used…asked at least one planned probe, and one spontaneous follow-up on an unexpected answer
Follow-ups…pursued an interesting answer before moving to the next question (follow the thread, then return to schedule)
Listening…let the interviewee finish; no interruptions; allowed silences instead of filling them
Neutrality…gave no “great!”, no frowns, no leading reactions; body language stayed even
Question discipline…asked one question at a time; rephrased instead of repeating jargon; kept to the schedule loosely (not robotically)
Talk ratio…talked ≤ 20% of the time; the interviewee did most of the talking
Common failure modes (observe and name these in feedback)

Double-barrelled spoken questions · suggesting answers (“Did you find it annoying, like most people?”) · interrogating yes/no chains · note-taking so heavy the eye contact dies · arguing with the participant · finishing their sentences · skipping the pre-interview data-use statement.

Activity 4 · Interview reflections

The template — every role answers: what worked / what to improve / what changes to the schedule
RoleModel reflection (what you'd say in the group)
Interviewer“The grand-tour opener worked — the participant narrated a full shopping trip and I got three probe-worthy details for free. What didn't work: I read Q3 while the participant was still answering Q2, so I missed the chance to ask about the shared family list she mentioned. Next time I'll mark 'must-pick-up' threads in the margin. I'd also reorder Q7 earlier — we ran out of time for the catch-all.”
Interviewee“I opened up most when asked 'walk me through your last shop' — concrete beats abstract. The question 'how do you feel about the supermarket knowing what you buy?' felt big and I wasn't sure what level to answer at; a probe like 'knowing what exactly?' would have helped me. When the interviewer nodded at my first answer I could feel myself performing 'savvy tech user'.”
Observer“Talk ratio was about 60/40 in the interviewee's favour — better than most first attempts, but the interviewer summarised answers back three times when a simple 'mm-hm, and then?' would have kept the flow. One question was double-barrelled ('which apps and why?'). Probes were used on 4 of 8 questions. The consent preamble was skipped in the second run — flag that next round.”

Schedule improvements that commonly emerge (say these and you sound experienced): merge questions that overlap · move the most productive grand-tour question earlier · mark 2–3 “flex” questions that can be dropped under time pressure · add a written prompt column for probes so the interviewer looks at the participant, not the page · put the consent preamble as item 0 on the schedule itself so it can't be forgotten.
