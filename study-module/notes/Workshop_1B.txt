Activity 1: Example 1 (after the initial discussion)
Dimensions of Research Ethics
Example 1: 
You are doing research on barista staff use a new coffee making machine. You are using a video 
to study their use in the shop, videoing staff members who have consented to take part in the 
study. Occasionally the video captures people who have not consented to take part in the study.
• Informed consent: not everyone videoed.
• Privacy: no consent to video some people.

Activity 1:  Example 2 (after the initial discussion)
Dimensions of Research Ethics
Example 2: 
You are doing a study of how people use Facebook to talk about politics. This involves 
scraping comments from a public Facebook group and classifying them as “friendly”, “neutral” & 
“hostile”. When you publish examples of comments you use examples of “hostile” posts.
• Informed consent: have people consented?
• Confidentiality: people can find the name of authors of your “hostile” post examples.
• Psychological harm: authors of “hostile” posts put at risk of psychological abuse.

Sample answers
Ethical dimension Sample answer: hypothetical IT research 
scenario Why is it an ethical concern?
1. Physical or psychological harm or abuse
Researchers test a VR application that 
exposes participants to frightening or 
distressing scenarios without adequate 
warning or support.
Participants may experience anxiety, stress 
or psychological distress. Researchers 
should minimise foreseeable harm, warn 
participants about relevant risks, and allow 
them to stop the experiment.
2. Informed consent
Researchers collect participants' screen 
recordings, keystrokes and application 
usage without explaining these activities in 
the consent form.
Participants cannot make an informed 
decision if they do not understand what 
data will be collected, how it will be used, or 
what risks are involved.
3. Right of withdrawal
An experimental application does not allow 
participants to stop recording or withdraw 
their data once they have started the study.
Participation should be voluntary. 
Participants should understand how to 
withdraw, whether their data can still be 
removed, and any practical limitations after 
data have been irreversibly anonymised or 
published.
===== PAGE 20 =====
Sample answers
Ethical dimension Sample answer: hypothetical IT research 
scenario Why is it an ethical concern?
4. Vulnerable participant groups
Researchers test an educational AI 
application on children who may not 
understand how their personal information 
will be processed.
Children may have limited capacity to 
understand research risks or make 
independent decisions. Researchers should 
use age-appropriate explanations, seek 
appropriate parental or guardian consent, 
and obtain children's assent where 
appropriate.
5. Deception
Researchers tell participants that they are 
interacting with another student when they 
are actually interacting with an AI chatbot.
Participants are deliberately given 
misleading information, which may affect 
their decisions or trust. Deception requires 
justification, appropriate ethics approval, 
and debriefing when suitable.
6. Coercion
A lecturer tells students they must 
participate in a research experiment to 
receive good grades.
Students may feel unable to refuse 
because of the researcher's authority. 
Recruitment should make clear that 
participation is voluntary and will not affect 
grades or academic standing.
===== PAGE 21 =====
Sample answers
Ethical dimension Sample answer: hypothetical IT research 
scenario Why is it an ethical concern?
7. Illegal practices
Researchers ask participants to access a 
third-party computer system without 
permission to test its security.
The study may involve unauthorised access 
or other unlawful activities. Researchers 
should obtain appropriate authorisation and 
ensure the research complies with 
applicable laws and institutional policies.
8. Invasion of privacy
Researchers scrape private social media 
messages or continuously record 
participants' computer screens without 
adequate justification or permission.
Personal information and private activities 
may be exposed beyond what participants 
reasonably expect. Data collection should 
be necessary, proportionate and 
appropriately disclosed.
9. Confidentiality
Researchers publish a dataset containing 
participant names, email addresses and 
identifiable workplace activity logs.
Participants could be identified, 
embarrassed or harmed. Researchers 
should restrict access, remove direct 
identifiers where possible, protect the data 
and control how results are shared.

and make “opt-in”, rather than targeting 
specific people (coercion).

Activity 2 (Example)
Reviewing Actual Research for Ethical Issues 
ASPECT OF RESEARCH DESIGN: inappropriately capture people and audio in during the activity, there 
are many reasons why this might be inappropriate: (i) additional people not consented; (ii) content might 
be sensitive; (iii) there might be a conflict of interest between a person paid to do the annotation and the 
person or process being discussed.
AUTHOR DESCRIPTION: “a keyboard which allowed the user to temporarily disable recording if they felt 
their conversations were too sensitive to be recorded (to ensure privacy, subjects could also retroactively 
request the recordings from any time period be destroyed prior to viewing).”
MY PROPOSAL: the durations of the recording sessions were very long, and the mechanism by which 
people could retroactively have recordings is not clear. Instead, use shorter recording sessions and ask 
subjects to review recording and remove elements they think are inappropriate before giving to the 
research team.
===== PAGE 27 =====
Activity 2 (Example)
Reviewing Actual Research for Ethical Issues 
ASPECT OF RESEARCH DESIGN: Duration of recordings for each subject are very long. The idea that 
all activities are recorded on video and reviewed by researcher is likely to cause psychological harm 
(stress). Also given the number of subjects is small the perceived “damage” (to the study) of withdrawing 
from the study is high (more stress).
AUTHOR DESCRIPTION: “7am to 6pm on workdays for 14-22 days for each subject” but no discussion of 
the stress that this is likely to cause subjects.
MY PROPOSAL: use more subjects, for shorter periods, allow them to review footage, continuously 
check that they are happy to continue (by asking for their consent at each session).

Sample answers
Research aspect Potential ethical concern What the authors report How I would address it
1. Long-term audio and video recording
Recording employees throughout their 
working day may expose private 
conversations, work habits, visitors and 
sensitive workplace information.
The authors describe using a camera and 
microphone in each office. Participants 
could temporarily disable recording and 
retrospectively request that recordings from 
a specified period be destroyed before the 
recordings were viewed. 2003_Predicting-
human-Oz.pdf
I would clearly explain the recording 
arrangements, minimise recording to what is 
necessary, provide an obvious 
pause/disable mechanism, and establish a 
procedure to exclude sensitive 
conversations from analysis.
2. Informed consent and transparency
Participants need to understand the extent 
of the recording and the fact that 
researchers will manually inspect 
recordings.
The paper describes the recording and 
manual-coding process, but the sections 
provided do not clearly document the 
consent procedure or exactly what 
participants were told about the manual 
analysis. 2003_Predicting-human-Oz.pdf
I would provide a participant information 
statement and obtain documented informed 
consent before recording. The explanation 
should cover camera and microphone use, 
manual review, data access, potential risks 
and withdrawal options.
3. Right of withdrawal
Participants may wish to stop recording 
during sensitive discussions or remove 
recordings they later consider inappropriate.
Participants could disable recording 
temporarily and request retrospective 
destruction of recordings before they were 
viewed. The paper does not clearly establish 
the full procedure for withdrawing from the 
entire study. 2003_Predicting-human-Oz.pdf
I would provide a clearly documented 
withdrawal process, explain applicable 
deadlines and data-removal limitations, and 
log and action deletion requests.
4. Psychological harm and disruption
Frequent audio prompts could interrupt 
concentration, disrupt conversations or 
increase participants' workload.
The authors state that they used a single 
question and a five-point scale to minimise 
disruption. They averaged about two 
prompts per hour. 2003_Predicting-human-
Oz.pdf
I would allow participants to skip prompts, 
offer adjustable prompt frequency, and 
provide the option to pause participation 
during important tasks or meetings.

Sample answers
Research aspect Potential ethical concern What the authors report How I would address it
5. Privacy of visitors and other third parties
Office visitors could be recorded even 
though they were not the main research 
participants.
The paper describes cameras positioned to 
capture the main work areas and the office 
door. The researchers also coded 
information about guests and their 
activities. The sections provided do not 
clearly explain how visitor consent was 
handled. 2003_Predicting-human-Oz.pdf
2003_Predicting-human-Oz.pdf
I would prevent or minimise recording of 
non-participants, pause recording during 
sensitive meetings, and establish a clear 
process for informing visitors and handling 
any inadvertently captured data.
6. Confidentiality and data security
Audio and video can identify participants 
through faces, voices, conversations and 
working patterns. Multiple coders reviewing 
recordings increases the number of people 
with access.
Student coders were employed and trained 
for consistency. A randomly selected 5% of 
the data received duplicate coding, yielding 
93.4% agreement. This addresses coding 
consistency, but does not by itself 
demonstrate how confidentiality or secure 
storage was managed. 2003_Predicting-
human-Oz.pdf
I would restrict raw recording access to 
authorised team members, require 
confidentiality agreements, encrypt stored 
files, keep identity keys separate from 
analysis data, and establish retention and 
secure-deletion schedules.
7. Treatment of missing self-reports
Automatically assigning a response to 
someone who did not answer may 
misrepresent their actual state.
Of 672 prompts, 54 received no response 
while the participant was present. The 
authors investigated these cases and 
classified them as least interruptible for 
modelling; they also reported that removing 
them had little effect on accuracy. 
2003_Predicting-human-Oz.pdf
I would retain these responses as missing 
unless there were sufficient evidence to 
justify another classification. I would 
conduct a sensitivity analysis comparing 
alternative treatments and transparently 
document the decision.

Step 1. Recruitment
Ethical considerations: Participation must be voluntary. Staff should not feel pressured to participate 
because of their employment relationship or the authority of the researcher. Managers should not know 
who declined unless there is a justified, approved reason.

Ethical considerations: Only authorised researchers should access identifiable recordings. The coding scheme should be applied consistently, analytical decisions should 
be documented, and the results should be reported honestly. For example, a missing self-report should not automatically be assumed to mean that a participant was not 
interruptible without justification and consideration of its impact on the results.
