 Overview
            

Literature and Configuring Research

This week, we will explore 
literature search and how to configure your research. You will learn how to do literature review and structure it in an informative way. Then in Part B, you will learn how to frame a piece of research you want to conduct, and 
investigate if there is existing knowledge that you can learn from, or can inform your approach.

Week's Learning Outcomes:

Identify the purpose of a literature review and describe the role of the literature review in providing the reader with a context.

Select an appropriate literature review format and structure for their research context.

Describe the main elements of a good literature review.

Employ techniques to carry out a literature review efficiently.

Define the scope of a literature review.

Know how to search for information and assess its quality.

Know how to cite and reference sources correctly.

Understand the constraints, criteria and resources that impact a research study.

Recognise the different value of qualitative and quantitative approaches to data collection and how they can work together.

Demonstrate an understanding of how a research study can impact on how a research question is answered.

Introduction

Whether you are doing research in industry or academia, for an existing product or speculative experiment, it���s always important to know what else might exist that can inform your own research. Both companies and academic institutions create searchable literature by creating documents such as ���white papers���, ���journal articles��� and ���conference proceedings���. Sometimes it can also be useful to look at official documentation or marketing material for products or magazine or news articles. Developing an inclusion criterion that is best suited for your context is key.
Conducting a literature search allows you to learn from past mistakes, streamline your own knowledge creation by not asking the same questions and gain context into what you are asking and why. In many cases its also helpful to have this context when writing about your research in your own reports, white papers or academic articles.

Conducting a Literature Search
: Conducting a literature search is key to understanding existing knowledge (so you do not end up asking questions that have already been answered), and informing you of appropriate methods and best practice.

Case Study

Margherita works at Apple as a senior mobile developer. She manages a team of 5-7 people, and her team consists of developers and testers. Margherita���s team focuses on developing and designing new mobile products and applications for their core application suite. Margherita���s team was tasked with developing and testing an algorithm for collecting and identifying various physical activity movements from users suffering from chronic pain as an add-on for the Fitness and Health product. The algorithm has to be time-efficient and should consume little battery. Margherita and her team need to develop their ideas by researching state-of-the-art technologies that are currently available on the market. This will help them ensure they are up to date with the existing technology and avoid designing redundant products and repeating what exists on the market by their competitors, such as Fitbit, which could lead to legal implications for the company. Margherita and her team decide to conduct a Competitive Market Analysis to research major competing products and determine what potential competition their product would have. The team would compile a list of the major competing products available on the market, and come up with a set of criteria to compare them. The criteria included the types of physical movement the products detected, the time it took them to identify the movement, their prices, and marketing strategies used by their competitors to sell these products.
Moreover, the team would need to identify the latest research that would be relevant to their new product, both to understand what was possible to achieve, and what was already known about such algorithms. They would scrape the web and collect research articles published in high-impact journals and conferences by combining keywords such as ���movement detection algorithms��� and ���chronic pain patients���. They would rank the articles against a set of criteria and determine what (if any) useful features (such as detecting when the patient is swimming) they could draw on, and what were missing from existing research which they would need to develop themselves.

Introduction

So far we have covered the key steps you could undertake to frame a piece of research you want to conduct, and investigate if there is existing knowledge that you can learn from, or can inform your approach. We also covered what ethical issues and responsibilities should be considered when planning a piece of research.

The next part of the process is where things really start to happen - planning what your research will actually look like in practice. This process draws on the previous 3 steps in terms of framing, planning and risk, and leads into how to plan for a ���study���. Operationalizing research can be a complex process, particularly if it���s not planned well, so it���s key to spend time understanding how you want to conduct your research, who is going to be involved and what resources you require.

As you might have realised by now, a key part of this process involves making decisions on the type, modality and scale of any data you might want to collect. In most cases, a single research question can be answered in a number of different ways, each requiring different types (or combinations) of data. Choosing how and what your data is going to be is a key part of designing your study, particularly as conducting such activities will have resource and financial implications.

This week, we are going to explore the factors you might want to consider during this planning process, and various options and strategies you can deploy to design an effective study.

About the Workshop

Workshops are delivered either online using Zoom or in-person on campus. Your lecturers/tutors will have contacted you to introduce themselves and to share the Zoom link (if applicable) for the weekly workshop.

Contact the lecturer if you have any questions about the timing of a workshop or require a Zoom link for your workshop. This information is also available on Moodle.

Pre-workshop material

Each workshop is a collaborative space designed to give you a space to explore and practice new research skills. You will participate in activities that are based directly on the pre-material on this page. Before this weeks workshop you should:

Consume

You need to watch the videos (before the workshop): These aim to present content in small (5-10 minute) chunks and are subtitled.

Reflect

You should review the content: Slides and transcripts are provided for all the videos, reading these can help you understand the content in your own time.

Engage

You should engage with the associated reading: There are various resources and papers, that we'll be pointing you to before and during Week 2, so take some time to review these.

Participate

Post into the discussion board: if you have questions or insights that you want to share please don't hesitate to post these, (also if you have any questions about the unit).
