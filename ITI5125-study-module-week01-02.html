WEEK 1 Research methods & ethics — roadmap

Covers: lecture slides "What is research?" + "Introduction to research ethics", Workshop 1A (why research methods), Workshop 1B (ethics), Hudson et al. 2003, Beneteau et al. 2019, UX Collective qual/quant article.

1.1What week 1 wants you to walk away with

Week 1 answers three big questions:

  • What actually is "research"? — a systematic enquiry that discovers new information or verifies existing knowledge, for a specified purpose (definitions from Bennett 1991 and Leedy & Ormrod 2013).
  • Why does IT need its own research methods? — because IT problems live in messy real-world systems, and every method is flawed in its own way; choosing and defending a method is a core professional skill.
  • What makes research ethical? — the historical codes (Nuremberg, Helsinki), the four values (merit & integrity, justice, beneficence, respect), and nine practical dimensions you must spot in any study.
Exam anchor: week 1 quizzes love (a) definitions of research, (b) naming the correct method for a scenario, (c) matching a study design to an ethical dimension, and (d) details from the two assigned papers — especially the Hudson Wizard-of-Oz study, which the ethics workshop dissects line by line.

1.2The week 1 content map

Everything feeds the same quiz: lectures give concepts, papers give case material, workshops give scenarios.

1.3Why research at all?

The lecture frames research as a rewarding activity with four payoffs: solve problems and improve the human condition (the traditional university role — think of Hooke's microscope and everything that followed), generate new knowledge that drives innovation and prosperity (the modern commercial role), and provide intellectual challenge. The quiz can ask you to pick which of these is not a stated reason — so anchor on the four.

Exam trap: "Research is any reading or searching" is wrong. The defining word in every definition is systematic — a careful, organised enquiry to discover or verify knowledge for a specified purpose.

If a quiz option says research = "proving a point you already believe" or "random exploration with no method", eliminate it. Research must be systematic and purpose-driven.

2.2Theory, observation and experiment

Methods differ, but almost all research relies on observation. The lecture draws the classic loop:

The lecture's example is special relativity: the theory predicted that fast-moving clocks run slow; the Rossi–Hall experiment observed muons (created by cosmic rays, travelling >0.99c) decaying slower than their half-life at two altitudes — Echo Lake (3,240 m) vs Denver (1,616 m). The key exam point: the observation confirmed a novel, previously unobserved phenomenon predicted by theory — that's the gold standard of evidence.

2.4Science vs engineering (and basic vs applied)

Science
  • Creates knowledge; studies the world as it is
  • Trained in the scientific method; designs experiments to test theories
  • Uses explicit knowledge; "thinkers"
Engineering
  • Applies knowledge; seeks to change the world
  • Trained in engineering design; designs artefacts using theories
  • Prefers contingent knowledge; "doers"

Related axis: basic research expands knowledge without an immediate use; applied research targets a practical problem. A study can sit on either axis — "applied + engineering" describes most IT industry research.

2.5The method menu — and why every method is flawed

The lecture lists the classic IT methods: observation, experiment, design, simulation, survey, interview, focus group, case study, action research. Different contexts need different methods; no method is adequate for all contexts, and modern research is increasingly interdisciplinary — which is exactly why you must be able to critically assess quality and limitations.

MethodSignature flaw from the lecture
Lab experimentCannot study large-scale software development in the lab; too many variables to control
Case studyDoes one project generalise? Researcher picks the questions → bias
SurveySelf-selection bias; respondents tell you what they think you want to hear
Exam trap: "all methods are flawed" does not mean "all methods are equally bad". The tested skill is matching a flaw to the right method and saying how you'd mitigate it (triangulate with a second method).

2.6Qualitative vs quantitative — the article's version

The UX Collective article (saved from week 1) states the split in plain product-design terms — and it mirrors the lecture exactly:

Qualitative

Answers why & how. Listen to real users, observe behaviour, analyse lived experience. Small samples; it's about insights, not numbers. Examples: moderated A/B walkthroughs, scenario walkthroughs ("buy a cabbage"), intercepts at a café, field visits.

Quantitative

Answers how many / how much. Numbers that generalise: completion rates, conversion, drop-off per step, usage distribution. Larger samples — the article cites the Nielsen Norman rule of thumb of 20+ users per design option as a budget/science balance.

The article's one-line thesis worth memorising: "the design of your research might be more important than the implementation of it" — you pick methods to prove or disprove a hypothesis (an initial assumption from limited information), and you often mix: qualitative interviews to form hypotheses, quantitative tests to check they scale — or big data first to find pain points, then qualitative to learn why.

WEEK 1 Research ethics

Explains the lecture "week-02-part-01 introduction (research ethics)" and feeds directly into Workshop 1B, where every concept below is applied to real papers.

3.1What "ethics" covers in research

Ethics is wider than consent forms. The lecture lists the full territory you must be aware of in any research community:

  • Ethics of conducting research (how you treat participants)
  • Ownership and custodianship of data (who holds it, who may see it, for how long)
  • Copyright and intellectual property
  • Other standards of behaviour (integrity, honesty in reporting)

New technologies sharpen these issues — the lecture flags AI (transparency in decision-making) and data science (amplification of bias). Paper 2 in week 2 (underdiagnosis bias) is literally the "amplification of bias" example — ethics concepts will follow you all unit.

3.2Where modern research ethics comes from

Nuremberg Code (1947)

Written after WWII atrocities in human experimentation. Its absolute rule: informed consent is essential; participants must be free to stop at any time; only qualified researchers; there must be a favourable risk/benefit ratio.

Declaration of Helsinki (1960)

World Medical Association. Key line: "the well-being of the subject should take precedence over the interests of science and society." Consent should be in writing; extra caution when participant is in a dependent relationship with the researcher.

Exam trap: the codes are easy to swap. Nuremberg = 1947 = consent is absolutely essential + risk/benefit ratio. Helsinki = 1960 = subject's well-being over science + written consent + dependent relationships. If an option mixes them, it's wrong.

WEEK 1 · PAPER Predicting human interruptibility — a Wizard of Oz study

Hudson, Fogarty, Atkeson, Avrahami, Forlizzi, Kiesler, Lee & Yang (2003), CHI 2003, pp. 257–264. This is the "simulation study" the week 1 ethics workshop dissects — expect the quiz to know it in detail.

4.1The problem in one breath

Humans can glance through an open office door and know "bad time to interrupt". Computers are "almost entirely oblivious" to the human world and interrupt blindly, burning attention. The paper asks: can simple sensors predict a person's interruptibility — and which sensors matter most?

4.2Why "Wizard of Oz"?

Building real sensors first would mean betting effort on sensors that might turn out ill-suited. Instead the researchers simulated a whole menu of plausible sensors: they recorded offices with an ordinary camera + microphone, then had human coders watch the recordings and "act as" the sensors — logging events like talking, on-phone, keyboard use, guests present, door closed — and trained predictive models on those simulated readings.

Wizard of Oz (research sense): the "intelligent" system is secretly powered by a human. You get the data a smart sensor would produce, without building the sensor — perfect for feasibility studies. The ethics cost: humans are watching real recordings of real people, which is exactly where the privacy issues come from (workshop 1B).

4.3Study design — the numbers the quiz loves

4
subjects
602 h
video/audio recorded
672
self-report prompts
  • Who: 4 high-level university staff with similar jobs, private offices, doors usually open (closed in the 5 min before a sample only 3% of the time — removing the "door closed = don't disturb" cue and making prediction harder).
  • Capture: PC + camera + mic per office; grayscale 320×240 at ~6 fps, 8-bit audio at 11 kHz; recorded 7am–6pm on workdays, 14–22 days per subject.
  • Ground truth: experience sampling ("beeper study") — on average 2 prompts/hour, asking one 5-point question: "rate your current interruptibility, 1 = most interruptible", answered by holding up fingers (mostly answered verbally). 672 prompts collected when the subject was present.
  • Missing data: 54 of 672 (8.0%) prompts got no response — subjects were usually on the phone or with a guest; the authors classified these as least interruptible (later shown to barely affect accuracy).

4.4From recordings to 128 simulated sensors

Coders logged 23 event types (occupant presence; speaking/writing/sitting/standing/on-phone; touching desk, keyboard, mouse, monitor-gaze, papers, food, drink; guest count and guest behaviour; door open/closed; day and hour). Each event was then expanded into time-window variants, e.g. Imm (in the 15 s around the prompt), All-1 / Any-1 (every/at-least-one 15 s interval in the prior minute), All-5 / Any-5 (same over 5 minutes), Count-5 (how many intervals in 5 min). Result: 128 simulated sensors (98 binary + 30 counts).

Which single sensors predict best? An information gain ranking (relative entropy removed when sorting data by that feature) put occupant talk and telephone in 8 of the top 9 positions — then sitting/standing, guest talking, keyboard and monitor use. Short windows (Imm, Any-1, All-1) generally beat 5-minute windows; Count-5 matched the short windows.

4.5The models and the headline result

The task was made binary: predict "least interruptible" (5) vs anything else (1–4) — motivated by the real use case (avoiding the most harmful interruptions) and by the self-reports being bimodal. Four machine-learning techniques, all from open-source packages (C4.5, Weka):

ModelAccuracy (10× 90/10 cross-validation)
Decision trees (C4.5, min branch 15)78.1% ← best; base rate was 68.0%
Support vector machines77.8%
AdaBoost with decision stumps76.9%
Naïve Bayesian75.0%

The similar results across very different algorithms are the paper's evidence that prediction is feasible, not a fluke of one technique.

4.6Model variations — three results to remember

  • Personalised models didn't help: per-subject trees scored 69.1 / 81.9 / 74.6 / 76.0 — no consistent gain, blamed on far less training data.
  • Threshold 3 instead of 4 (redefining "not interruptible" as ≥3): accuracy fell to 69.6% — the binary split at 4 is the right one.
  • Five-way prediction (predict the raw 1–5 score) was much harder: 45.1% exact, 72.6% within one. But mapped back to binary it gave 74.9% accuracy with only 10.4% incorrect interruptions — a model you can tune at run-time with a conservative threshold (a "never interrupt" dial), trading some accuracy for far fewer rude interruptions.

4.7The easy-sensor punchline

A final model built only from easy-to-build sensors — "anyone talking" (no need to tell occupant from guest), telephone, keyboard, mouse, time of day — reached 76.5%. Conclusion: robust 75–80% interruptibility prediction is realistic with simple sensors, and speech detectors are the single most promising sensor.

Exam trap: 78% is the decision-tree accuracy; 68% is the base rate (always guessing "interruptible"). Quizzes love offering "the model beat humans" — the paper never claims that; it compares models to each other and to the base rate only.

WEEK 1 · PAPER Communication breakdowns between families and Alexa

Beneteau, Richards, Zhang, Kientz, Yip & Hiniker (2019), CHI 2019. A qualitative field study blending HCI with speech-language pathology.

5.1The setup in one breath

Voice assistants are everywhere (projected in 55% of US households by 2022; 93% of children in one study had used voice tech), yet humans do all the adapting — we shorten sentences, simplify, repeat — and breakdowns pile up. This study watches real families deal with those breakdowns at home, and asks: what breaks, how do families repair it, and how does Alexa herself respond?

5.2Method — the numbers

10
families
4 wks
home deployment
59
breakdowns analysed
  • Who: 10 economically and ethnically diverse families, none previously owned a digital home assistant, household income at/below county median, each with ≥1 child aged 4–17; 2 families bilingual (English/Spanish).
  • Procedure: each home got an Echo Dot (2nd gen) for 4 weeks. Pre- and post-deployment interviews in the home; compensation US$100 in gift cards; families kept the device; IRB-approved.
  • Capture: custom audio-buffer system (built on open-source code from Porcheron et al.) — whenever the trigger word "Alexa" was spoken, it saved 1 minute before + 3 minutes after = 4-minute clips to a secure server, via a study Samsung tablet placed next to the Dot. Families had a "record a thought" button and a "delete" button — a deliberate ethics feature (compare week 1 ethics: privacy, withdrawal, confidentiality).
  • Analysis: inductive — memoing + open coding during transcription of all 10 families' audio; 14.5 hours reviewed; corpus of 59 communication-breakdown interactions, max 3 instances per code per family. Coding was validated by two independent speech-language pathologists (SLPs) — the first author is herself an SLP.

5.4Three breakdowns that show the theory working

Family E — the popsicles (direct instruction + modeling, then abandoning Alexa)

An 8-year-old asks "what are popsicles made of?"; Alexa mishears as s'mores popsicles. The mother first scaffolds her son ("she thinks it's s'mores popsicles because you didn't articulate" — direct instruction), then models a repair herself ("how do you make a popsicle?"). Alexa replies on-topic but too specific, offers "say next" — itself a form of scaffolding — the parents get enough, stop Alexa, and finish teaching their son themselves. Lesson: the family carries the repair burden; the human topic outlives the device.

Family B — "is South Korea in East or Southeast Asia?" (five repairs, three people)

The mother consults family, tries Alexa; neutral response. The daughter contracts to a yes/no question; Alexa beeps and goes silent — the most dramatic breakdown. Mother instructs her to try again; daughter repeats louder; another neutral response. Mother splits the either/or question into two closed questions; Alexa answers about Eastern Europe (acting on misunderstanding). Only when the father — listening all along — raises his volume and redirects ("Please define East Asian countries") does Alexa answer. Then Alexa does something rare: asks "Did that answer your question?" — turn-taking. Lesson: neutral responses give no signposts, so families iterate blindly; specific responses guide repair.

Family D — bilingual home + a 5-year-old's pronunciation

The child can't yet produce /l/ and /s/ consistently (normal at age 5 — both sounds are in "Alexa") and strings three questions into one utterance. The mother teaches pronunciation (direct instruction), simplifies the question (syntax + semantics), and when Alexa still fails, asks "what did I ask you?" — neutral response, everyone laughs, then a final contracted yes/no question succeeds. A second theme: Alexa can't code-switch — asked in Spanish phonology for "Marco Antonio Solís" it returns a wrong English-artist match; it reads "add 157…" literally instead of inferring "calculate". Lesson: children under ~5 struggle most; Alexa lacks pragmatics — the social use of language.

5.5Findings & design recommendations

  • The burden of repair sits with the family — multiple members, multiple attempts; neutral clarification responses are far more common than specific ones and don't help humans repair.
  • Design for artificial code-switching: detect who is speaking (child → expect repetition and imprecise speech) and adapt responses accordingly.
  • Use discourse scaffolding + specific clarification responses ("Can you tell me just the key word of what you want?") — more signposts than "Sorry, I'm not sure".
  • Exploit joint media engagement: design for the group, not the individual — invite others in ("Is there anyone else who can jump in and help?") and let the assistant model meta-pragmatic skills.

The paper closes by returning to Suchman (1987): machines should be designed for collaboration toward shared understanding — the burden of understanding should not default to the human.

Exam trap: NR vs SR is the most testable distinction — both are "clarification responses", but only the specific one ("Did you mean X?") actually helps repair. Also note this is a qualitative study (59 breakdowns, coding, SLP validation) — don't call it an experiment.

WEEK 1 · WORKSHOP Workshop 1A solved — OnStudent feature development

Week 1A workshop sheet + workshop slides. Scenario: OnStudent, a student-administration platform, wants to add "family engagement" to onboarding. Three activities walk you through a mini research pipeline: people → system → communication.

6.1Activity 1 — understanding people (design an interview study)

The task: interview students who used OnStudent, to learn how family engagement should be introduced.

DecisionModel answer + the reasoning the quiz wants
Who to interview?Students who recently completed onboarding using OnStudent (memory reliable), mixed across demographics (year level, domestic/international, mode of study) so you don't only hear one kind of student.
Where to recruit?OnStudent's existing client universities (with their permission), via the platform's own channels + student newsletters — the population that actually used the product.
How many?Qualitative logic, not statistics: roughly 6–12 interviews is a defensible range — enough for saturation (new interviews stop adding new themes). This is an insight study, not a measurement study.
How long?30–45 minutes each — long enough to build rapport and go deep, short enough to recruit and schedule within the project.

Three things to find out from each student:

  • How does the student currently keep family informed during onboarding, and who initiates it?
  • What friction or concerns would family involvement create (privacy, autonomy, information overload)?
  • Under what conditions would family engagement feel helpful rather than intrusive (control over what's shared, opt-in)?

Method to turn interviews into reportable knowledge: semi-structured interviews, recorded (with consent), transcribed and thematically coded (thematic analysis) — quote-supported themes feed the report. Note the contrast you'll see again in week 2: interviews = qualitative = why.

6.2Activity 2 — understanding a system (quantitative data)

The setup: a prototype auto-matches family members by demographic profile so they can chat. The platform logs everything: timestamps of all communication, demographic profiles (location, ethnicity, income range, age range), and message content. The CEO wants to know the feature's impact on student–university communication.

1 · What impact, and how to show it? Define metrics before touching data: e.g. student→university message frequency and response latency, time-on-platform, completion of onboarding tasks, complaint rates — compared between the 20% of clients with the new feature and matched clients without (comparative / A-B style analysis). The workshop slides flag "comparative study" as the right design family here.

2 · Which logged data demonstrates it? Timestamps → frequency/latency trends per cohort; message content → topic coding (how much communication shifts to family threads); demographics → check the matchmaking is balanced and whose families engage.

3 · The 20% bad timestamps twist: if 20% of timestamps are wrong with no way to reconstruct, any temporal analysis is biased — you can't trust ordering, durations or latency for that slice. Mitigation: quantify the corruption (is it random or systematic?), analyse the clean 80% separately, treat corrupted records as missing (not zero), and avoid fine-grained timing conclusions overall; triangulate with interview/survey data. The general lesson: data quality bounds your claims.

4 · One extra logging metric to build? The strongest answer is a cohort/feature identifier plus a direct engagement measure — e.g. a per-user flag linking each interaction to the feature version and whether the student's family was matched — because without a clean link between exposure (feature on/off) and behaviour, the "impact" comparison can't be computed at all. (Correlated data without a causal link is the recurring trap.)

6.3Activity 3 — communicating results (2-minute video)

The situation: tests show the auto-match feature reduced engagement; your interviews support a different proposal — trained volunteer "mentor families" matched by language and timezone. You must convince an internal review team and an external company, via a 2-minute video.

Model structure (about 4 × 25–30 s):

  • Hook + problem — one sentence: auto-matching backfired; engagement and complaints moved the wrong way.
  • Evidence — 1–2 headline numbers from the logs + one or two anonymised interview quotes (with permission) that explain why.
  • The proposal — mentor-family model: who mentors are, matching criteria, training, safeguards (student opt-in, moderation).
  • Ask + next step — approve a scoped pilot with success metrics; name the decision you need.

Presenting qualitative data on camera: short verbatim quotes as on-screen text (faces blurred/anonymised or voiced by actors), one simple journey diagram instead of tables. Technical depth: low for the external company (what & why, not how); the review team can get an appendix. Visuals: before/after engagement chart, 3-step mentor-matching diagram, quote cards. Production: scripted voice-over + screen capture of the prototype + stock/self-shot b-roll; tools like simple video editors or slide-to-video export.

Exam trap: when communicating to a mixed technical/non-technical audience, the correct choice is almost always "lead with findings and implications, keep method detail minimal, offer detail separately" — not "show all the statistics" and not "drop the evidence".

7.3Activity 3 — processes, people & documentation

The task: repeating Hudson's study at Monash with 20 staff means writing an ethics proposal. The workshop's sample answer decomposes the study into 4 processes × sub-processes × people × documents:

WEEK 2 Introduction to literature reviews

Explains the lecture "Week-03-part-01: Introduction to Literature Reviews" (the naming is odd — it's the literature lecture inside your week 2 folder).

9.1What a literature review does

A review is not a book report. It provides the background needed to understand a study (terminology, techniques), and explains the theoretical and practical basis for it:

  • the questions underlying the project,
  • the important issues, needs and gaps,
  • the theoretical and practical framework the study sits in.

Three moves with published work: synthesise what's relevant → summarise recent advances and their implications (the "state of the art") → explore the boundaries of previous work (what's been done, what hasn't).

9.2The three types of review

TypePurposeKey fact
Context for a study (paper, thesis)Organised around what's relevant to your problem; supports your research projectMost student reviews are this type
Survey of a fieldDefines the latest state of the field (systematic review) or resolves conflicting evidenceDoesn't support further research as part of the process — though it can set agendas
Review of reviews (meta-review)Reviews other reviews / meta-analysesUsed where the body of work is huge (e.g. health); example cited: Wilson et al. 2019 on self-regulation & medication adherence
Exam trap: a systematic literature review is a kind of survey — its job is mapping the field, not supporting one specific project. If an option says a systematic review exists to "support your study's design", that's the context review's job.

9.3Chronological vs thematic

Chronological

Group sources by publication date; shows how ideas developed. Weakness: slow to reach the newest, most important concepts. Lecture's verdict: not generally considered appropriate in IT/CS.

Thematic

Group by themes/concepts you (or the key studies) identify. More synthesis, latest research can appear anywhere, you control the order. The lecture notes students often start chronological while learning the field, then transition to thematic as their conceptual model matures.

Common practice in the final write-up: thematic structure with chronological ordering inside themes — a hybrid.

9.5Sourcing and assessing quality — the studio's version

The studio guide (Week 2B) turns "how do I find and judge papers" into three practical moves, all practised on the X-ray papers in the studio section:

  • Backward snowballing: read the abstracts of papers cited by your seed paper (via Google Scholar).
  • Forward snowballing: find papers that cite your seed paper, read their abstracts.
  • Quality indicators: the venue (is it a strong, peer-reviewed outlet?), citations (how much, and who cites it, where), and the authors (track record in the area).

WEEK 2 · READING Mixed methods (SAGE encyclopedia chapter)

Pinto, R. M. (2012), "Mixed Methods", in Encyclopedia of Research Design, SAGE. A dense chapter — here's the digest.

10.1The core idea

Mixed methods research integrates techniques from quantitative and qualitative paradigms to tackle questions that neither can answer alone. The chapter's claim: every research process should, through practical necessity, include aspects of both — the design of a study should follow the actual needs and purposes of the research problem.

10.2Qual vs quant — the paradigm contrast

QualitativeQuantitative
LogicInductive; constructivism; socially constructed realities; meaning & processDeductive; positivism/postpositivism; hypothesis testing; variables & causality
DataTextual; real-life settings; open-ended collection; narrative analysisNumerical; experiment, control groups, standardised collection; statistics
FocusEvents within contextMeasurements & frequencies across categories; correlations between variables

The chapter also coins two conversion moves: "qualitized" data = quantitative results converted into narratives analysed qualitatively; "quantitized" data = qualitative data converted into numeric codes and analysed statistically. Both directions exist; keep the terms straight.

10.3The history in four periods (Denzin & Lincoln)

10.4Paradigm wars → pragmatism

Purists once held that mixing paradigms would "corrupt" science. The counter-position — the compatibility thesis — holds they can be combined. The resolution the chapter champions is pragmatism: paradigms are socially constructed and malleable; methodological choices should be based on what works for the question in its context — "fitness for purpose". A dialectical stance treats opposing paradigms as valid perspectives that produce more realistic interaction.

10.5Multi-method, mixed methods, mixed model — Tashakkori & Teddlie

Design familyDefinition
Multi-method researchUses both qual and quant procedures, but applied principally within quantitative studies; often an interrelated series of theory-driven projects
Mixed methods researchThe umbrella of interest: qual + quant collection/analysis in parallel or sequential phases; the "mixing" shows up in the questions asked and the inferences drawn
Mixed model researchMixing happens in all stages — questions, methods, data collection, analysis, inferences

Plus one more axis: component designs keep the methods distinct for discrete parts of the research; integrated designs substantially integrate them.

Exam trap: multi-method ≠ mixed methods. Multi-method = both procedure types but mainly quantitative. Mixed methods = genuinely combining both paradigms. Mixed model = mixing at every stage.

10.6Triangulation and validity

Triangulation (from Denzin's The Research Act): determine an unknown point from two or more known points — i.e. collect data from different sources/methods to improve validity. A hypothesis explored under several methods is more valid than one tested by a single method. Convergence of results strengthens confidence; divergence isn't failure — it can reveal a more holistic picture and force productive modification of initial concepts.

The chapter closes honestly: mixed methods cost time and money, and integration must respect the integrity of each approach ("methodological congruence") so that neither process is corrupted.

WEEK 2 · WORKSHOP Configuring research — fully solved

Workshop 2A slides + "Week 2 Part B" activity sheet (the student-administration company scenario). All three activities are solved below, step by step, with the workshop's own model answers integrated.

11.1The scenario

You work at a company providing student-administration infrastructure to universities (enrolment → course/unit allocation → timetabling → transcripts → finance). The systems grew by absorbing other vendors' products, and nobody knows how well they work together. Your systems-integration team must find opportunities to improve: in the company's words, increased system efficiency and improved user experience. Findings go to the AGM in 3 months.

Two goals = two question types: efficiency is a what/where question (logs answer it) and UX is a why question (people answer it). Hold that split — it drives everything below.

11.2Activity 1 — focus, questions, data, methods (step-through)

Model answers (chosen focus: the student enrolment & timetabling journey — most-used, most visible, touched by students and admin staff):

  • RQ1 (UX / why): What friction points do students encounter during unit enrolment and timetabling, and how do these affect satisfaction and completion of enrolment?
  • RQ2 (efficiency / what): Where do delays, errors or drop-offs occur in the enrolment-to-timetabling workflow, as revealed by system usage logs?
  • Existing data (systems): server/app logs (response times, error rates, session drop-off), enrolment/timetabling records (clash rates, re-enrolment counts, processing time per transaction), helpdesk ticket categories.
  • Additional data from people: survey → breadth (satisfaction ratings, friction rankings); semi-structured interviews → depth (why students abandon enrolment); focus group → interaction effects (issues nobody raises alone).
  • Participants & criteria: students — currently enrolled, completed enrolment within the last 6 months, stratified by year level, international/domestic, full-time/part-time, ≥1 faculty per college (stratification stops you only hearing tech-comfortable students); admin staff — process enrolments daily, ≥2 departments (they carry the "efficiency" half of the brief).

WEEK 2 · PAPER 1 X-Raydar — open-source chest X-ray reading

Cid, Macpherson, Gervais-Andre, … Goh & Montana (2024). The Lancet Digital Health 6(1): e44–e57. Retrospective, multicentre study. This is the "build the AI" paper of the trio.

12.1Why this paper exists

Chest X-rays are ~40% of the 3.6 billion imaging procedures done worldwide each year; radiologist shortages create delays. Prior AI mostly detects one finding (pneumothorax, TB, nodules). This study builds comprehensive detection — 37 findings — and, crucially, open-sources everything (code, weights, web portal at x-raydar.info) so non-AI specialists can actually use it.

12.2The pipeline — how the system was built

Key design facts:

  • Data: 2,513,546 studies (2006–2019) from six UK hospitals in three NHS trusts → 1,940,508 usable free-text reports → 1,896,034 frontal images (only frontal; under-16s, lateral and damaged images excluded).
  • Taxonomy: 45 categories designed by 3 senior radiologists — 37 radiological findings across 8 anatomical areas, grouped by criticality (critical / urgent / non-urgent), plus 8 report-specific (technical) labels.
  • X-Raydar-NLP: RoBERTa-based label extractor trained semi-supervised on 23,230 manually annotated reports (27,781 sampled; tool = AnnotateX; 4,551 held out as test). It labels the whole dataset automatically — manual labelling of 1.9M reports was infeasible. Mean sensitivity 0.921, specificity 0.994, AUC 0.990 on the NLP test set.
  • X-Raydar (vision): multi-label classifier on InceptionV3, trained with a custom loss that incorporates label uncertainty from the NLP + class weighting by prevalence; final score = mean of models at 299², 512² and 1024² resolutions.

12.3The three test sets — know this table cold

Test setnHow labelledMean AUC (all findings)
Auto-labelled set103,328X-Raydar-NLP labels (same method as training)0.919 (0.039)
Consensus set1,427Two radiologists annotate each image; a third resolves disagreements (736 of 7,774 findings needed review; 14.75 min/image)0.864 (0.102)
MIMIC-CXR (external, US ER data)252,374X-Raydar-NLP, no fine-tuning0.842 (0.074)

The pattern — best on auto-labelled (same labelling method as training), lower on stricter/external sets — is exactly what you'd predict and a favourite quiz point: test-set construction changes the score. Strong results: critical findings AUC 0.943 on consensus; pneumothorax 0.974, pneumoperitoneum 0.981, subcutaneous emphysema 0.968, dextrocardia 0.997. Weak: paraspinal mass 0.468 (only n=3!), soft-tissue mass 0.651, cardiac calcification 0.752.

12.4AI vs the humans who wrote the reports

The paper's signature comparison: treat the historical radiologists' reports (written under real time pressure) as the human benchmark, and compare on the consensus set at a fixed 0.50 threshold:

  • Historic reporters: sensitivity 0.378, specificity 0.979, balanced accuracy 0.678.
  • X-Raydar: sensitivity 0.623, specificity 0.904, balanced accuracy 0.763 → +13.3% over humans.
  • Per finding: significantly superior on 27, non-inferior on 9, inferior on 1 (paraspinal mass). Gap narrows with criticality: +5.6% critical, +12.2% urgent, +16.9% non-urgent — explained by radiologists deliberately focusing on the clinical question and under-reporting minor findings.
Exam trap: the paper does not claim to beat radiologists in ideal conditions — it beats the historical, time-constrained reporting level, positioning X-Raydar as a second reader and a way to make reports more comprehensive. Also: sensitivity up / specificity down at 0.50 — trading false alarms for fewer misses is a chosen operating point, not an overall superiority.

12.5Generalisation, openness, limits

  • Generalisation: MIMIC-CXR performance held without fine-tuning despite a different country, setting (ER) and prevalence mix — evidence the preprocessing pipeline removed scanner effects; supports use as a foundation model.
  • Open science: full pre-trained weights (academic licence), web portal, APIs — aimed at resource-poor health systems.
  • Limitations: small consensus set (1,427) so some findings have tiny n; NLP labels carry noise (training was shown robust to it); model bias by ethnicity/sex/socioeconomic status not studied (data unavailable) — the exact gap Paper 2 attacks; only UK radiology practice considered.

WEEK 2 · PAPER 2 Underdiagnosis bias of chest X-ray AI

Seyyed-Kalantari, Zhang, McDermott, Chen & Ghassemi (2021). Nature Medicine 27: 2176–2182. This is the "audit the AI" paper — and a live case study in research ethics (bias amplification).

13.1The question

AI matches specialists "on average" — but average performance can hide systematically worse care for some groups. The paper studies underdiagnosis: the AI wrongly says a sick patient is healthy ("no finding"), so they get no treatment when they need it most. Definition you'll be tested on: underdiagnosis rate = the false-positive rate (FPr) of the 'No Finding' label — a false positive on "no finding" means "falsely declared healthy".

13.2Setup — four dataset settings

SettingSizeWhat it adds
CXR = MIMIC-CXR371,858 images / 65,079 patientsOnly dataset with race/ethnicity + insurance type
CXP = CheXpert223,648 / 64,740Sex, age only
NIH = ChestX-ray14112,120 / 30,805Sex, age; different hospital type (research hospital, selective admissions)
ALL707,626 / 129,819All three merged on 8 shared labels

Model: standard pipeline — DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet, trained on each dataset, averaged over 5 random seeds; mean AUCs 0.834 / 0.805 / 0.835 / 0.859. Deliberately "off the shelf" — the point is the data, not the model.

13.3The findings

  • Consistent, selective underdiagnosis: female, Black, Hispanic, under-20, and Medicaid-insured patients are falsely flagged healthy more often. (NIH flips — male and >80 there — likely small subgroups and its selective-admissions hospital.)
  • Intersectionality compounds it: belonging to two under-served groups stacks the penalty — e.g. Hispanic female patients underdiagnosed more than white female patients; 0–20 & female, 0–20 & Black, 0–20 & Medicaid worst of all.
  • FPr–FNr inverse relationship: under-served groups show high false-"healthy" rates without a matching rise in false-"sick" rates → selective underdiagnosis, not general noise. Underdiagnosis of the privileged groups is lowest.
  • Disease mix differs in underdiagnosed populations (proportionally more lung lesion, less pleural effusion) — detection is harder for some diseases.

13.4Why it happens and why it's hard to fix

  • Bias amplification: labels are extracted from clinical notes, and clinical care itself underdiagnoses these same groups — the model learns and magnifies the existing error. Labels are not neutral ground truth.
  • Automatic labelers need auditing — NLP labelers themselves show worse performance on under-represented subpopulations.
  • Post-hoc fairness is flawed: equalising FPr/FNr via per-group thresholds (where ROC curves cross) can require deliberately worsening a subgroup's performance — ethically unclear; and it needs group membership at decision time.
  • Fairness impossibility: with different base rates, you cannot simultaneously equalise FNR, FPR and false-discovery rate unless predictions are perfect. Fairness definitions must be chosen per use case — in triage, underdiagnosis is the fairness metric that matters.
The recommendation that ties week 1 to week 2: fairness checks for underdiagnosis should be part of regulatory approval of medical AI, evaluated during development and again after deployment. This is the "data science amplifies bias" ethics point from the week 1 lecture, with numbers.
Exam trap: underdiagnosis = "No Finding" false positive (sick predicted healthy) — not a false negative. The inverse FPr/FNr finding is the evidence against "it's just noisy data". And insurance type is used as an imperfect proxy for socioeconomic status — Medicaid ≈ low income.

WEEK 2 · PAPER 3 DCL — dynamic graphs + contrastive learning for report generation

Li, Lin, Chen, Lin, Liang & Chang (2023). IEEE/CVF CVPR 2023, pp. 3334–3343. The "go beyond labels, write the report" paper.

14.1The problem

Automatic radiology report generation should free radiologists from heavy workloads. Existing systems inject medical knowledge through fixed knowledge graphs — graphs pre-built from general knowledge (organs, ~20 disease keywords) that never update during training. Two failure modes: the fixed entities can't cover every dataset's common keywords (dataset bias), and clinically-linked entities from different organs can wrongly influence each other. DCL fixes the graph instead of the decoder.

14.2The DCL architecture (step-through)

14.3The dynamic graph, precisely

  • Base graph: extended from Zhang et al.'s pre-constructed graph — 28 entities ([CLS] global node + 7 organs/tissues + 20 disease keywords), all organs interlinked.
  • Case-specific update (bottom-up): for each image, retrieve the top-3 most similar reports (similarity of visual features against a report queue of 65,536 on MIMIC-CXR / 1,380 on IU-Xray) → extract anatomy/observation entities with Stanza → pull RadGraph triplets (relations: suggestive of, located at, modify) → add nodes or redefine relations per case.
  • Graph encoder: Transformer layer with relational self-attention — the adjacency matrix acts as a visible mask so a node only influences its linked nodes. Initialisation matters: SciBert embeddings + level encoding (root/organ/disease), not random — rare added nodes would never train from random init.
  • Graph attention: cross-attention where Q = visual features, K/V = graph features → the image feature that reaches the decoder is knowledge-enhanced.

14.4Contrastive learning objectives

  • IRC (image–report contrastive): positive image–report pairs pulled together, negatives pushed apart, over two momentum-encoder queues; learnable temperature.
  • IRM (image–report matching): binary task — is this pair real? Uses a multimodal encoder (training-only) with cross-attention.
  • Total loss = LRG (generation) + LIRC + LIRM. The contrastive losses also improve retrieval accuracy — which feeds back into graph quality.

14.5Experiments — datasets, metrics, results

IU-XrayMIMIC-CXR
Size3,955 reports / 7,470 images → 2069/296/590 train/val/test368,960 images / 222,758 reports (official split)

Metrics decoded (quiz fodder): NLG metrics measure descriptive accuracy — BLEU = word n-gram overlap (but biased CRG datasets let models score well by repeating frequent sentences!); CIDEr rewards topic terms and penalises frequent ones — hence the paper's pride in CIDEr. Clinical-efficacy metrics: CheXpert labels the generated and ground-truth reports on 14 medical terms, then precision/recall/F1 measure clinical correctness; 'Pleural Effusion' and 'Pleural Other' hit AUC 0.82.

Ablations: general knowledge alone +15.2% CIDEr over base; adding specific knowledge 0.535 → 0.557; IRC contributes more than IRM (directly aligns representations vs indirect); removing pretrained ViT collapses performance — Transformers need the pretrained inductive bias on small, unbalanced data.

Exam trap: CIDEr > BLEU is the paper's argument for quality — a model can game BLEU by parroting frequent sentences. If a question asks "which metric shows the model isn't just repeating the training set", the answer is CIDEr.

15.2Activity 2 — the literature search plan

Backward snowballing (cited references): e.g. from X-Raydar → Rajpurkar's CheXNet, the ChestX-ray14/NIH dataset paper, MIMIC-CXR dataset paper, CheXpert (Irving et al.), TorchXRayVision; from the bias paper → its fairness-metrics citations (equalised odds, FDR impossibility results); from DCL → R2Gen, KERP, MKG/PPKED, RadGraph.

Forward snowballing (citing papers): papers citing X-Raydar (later validation/foundation-model work), papers citing the bias paper (subsequent fairness-in-medical-AI audits), papers citing DCL (newer report-generation systems).
