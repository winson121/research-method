WEEK 4 Roadmap — qualitative analysis & descriptive statistics

What week 4 covers, why it matters, and how the two halves (making sense of words / making sense of numbers) fit your assignments.

Why this week exists

Weeks 1–3 taught you to collect data — ethically, from literature, via surveys and interviews. Week 4 is the pivot to analysis, and it comes in two flavours. Part A: you already have interview transcripts full of words; qualitative analysis is how you turn those words into defensible findings, and thematic analysis is the method this unit trains you in. Part B: numbers need the same discipline — descriptive statistics (summary statistics + charts) before any inferential claim, and the week's workshop runs the entire pipeline hands-on in MATLAB. The same principle runs through both halves: analysis is a craft with rules, not a vibe — codes must be evidenced in the text, and statistics must be the right ones for the data's level of measurement.

WEEK 4 · PART A Qualitative analysis — the landscape

Everything from the Part A introduction slides: why qualitative analysis, the seven common methods (with the one this unit focuses on), the working habits, and the challenge list.

Why qualitative analysis?

Qualitative analysis exists because some phenomena are difficult to measure precisely — experiences, perceptions, meanings, practices. Where quantitative data answers "how much / how many", qualitative data answers "why" and "how" — making sense of a phenomenon in context. Whether you need it depends on the context of your research and what you want to explore: if the interesting thing is what something means to people, numbers alone won't carry it.

Quantitative data

Numbers, counts, measurements. Precise, comparable, aggregable — but thin on meaning. Answered by statistics (this week's Part B onwards).

Qualitative data

Words, stories, observations, interactions. Rich with meaning — but messy, contextual, and needing disciplined interpretation. That discipline is this week's Part A.

The basics of qualitative data analysis (slide list)

  • It involves a range of methods — there is no single "qualitative analysis" (the seven below).
  • It is a hands-on approach for researchers — you are the instrument; software assists, it doesn't analyse.
  • A common practice: "analyse as you collect" — early analysis shapes later data collection (e.g. which follow-up interviews to do).
  • Software is available for data analysis — the slides list NVivo, ATLAS.ti and MAXQDA (dedicated qualitative data analysis packages), alongside general tools like SPSS; Excel can also code and thematically analyse data (Bree & Gallagher 2016).
  • There are different types of qualitative data analysis — pick to match your research question, not your comfort.

Overcoming challenges in qualitative analysis (slide list, adapted from SAGE)

Four named challenges:
  1. Protecting privacy, confidentiality and anonymity of participants — the week 1–2 ethics machinery (consent, de-identification, secure storage) applies directly to transcripts and quotes.
  2. Ensuring research integrity and quality — your codes and themes must be evidenced in the data (this is what separates analysis from opinion).
  3. Ownership of data — who holds the recordings and transcripts, and what may be done with them.
  4. Ethics overall — the umbrella over the other three.

WEEK 4 · PART A Thematic analysis decoded — Braun & Clarke and two worked examples

The method this unit trains: definition, the six phases, semantic vs latent, inductive vs theoretical — then the Maguire & Delahunt (2017) worked example and the Bartindale–Varghese coding walkthrough, both decoded step by step.

What thematic analysis is (and is not)

Thematic analysis is the process of identifying patterns or themes within qualitative data. Braun & Clarke (2006) suggest it is the first qualitative method a researcher should learn, because it provides core skills used by many other methods. The goal is to identify themes — patterns in the data that are important or interesting — and use them to address the research question. This is much more than summarising the data: a good thematic analysis interprets and makes sense of it.

Classic pitfall (Clarke & Braun 2013): using the main interview questions as the themes. If your "themes" are just your interview questions in disguise, you have summarised and organised the data — you have not analysed it. Themes must emerge from the codes, not from your schedule.

Two dials that define your analysis

DialPole APole B
DepthSemantic — codes/themes at the explicit, surface meaning of the data; nothing beyond what a participant said or wrote (B&C p.84)Latent — goes beneath the surface to the underlying ideas, assumptions, conceptualisations and ideologies shaping the semantic content
DirectionBottom-up / inductive — driven by the data itself; often line-by-line coding of everythingTop-down / theoretical — driven by the research question and/or the analyst's theoretical focus; code only what is relevant to the question

This week's workshop pushes you toward the latent pole: e.g. "I prefer written feedback…" → semantic code "preference for written feedback" vs latent code "written feedback adds a personal connection". And most unit examples are question-driven (top-down) — you code with an angle, not from a blank page.

The six phases of thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke 2006)

Worked example · Maguire & Delahunt (2017) — students' perceptions of feedback

Setup. Realist research question: "What are students' perceptions of feedback?" Data: an extract (~15 minutes) from one of a series of 8 focus groups with undergraduates, from a study on student perspectives on academic feedback. Because the question drives the analysis, the approach is top-down (theoretical), coding at the semantic level with open coding (no pre-set codes; codes develop and are modified as you work). It is deliberately a limited corpus — real analysis runs over weeks, not one extract.

Phase 1 — familiarisation. Read and re-read the transcript until very familiar; make notes and early impressions. Their rough notes: students think feedback is important but don't always find it useful; assessment including feedback can feel threatening and isn't always understood; students want very specific, personalised "how to improve" feedback; they want one-to-one discussion with lecturers (more personal, more private); the emotional impact of feedback matters.

Phase 2 — initial codes. Coding reduces lots of data into small chunks of meaning. They coded each segment relevant to the research question — not every line (line-by-line is the inductive option). Done by hand first (pens, highlighters on hard copies); software (ATLAS, NVivo) or even Excel (Bree & Gallagher 2016) helps with large sets; two coders are useful but not essential.

Phase 3 — search for themes. A theme is a pattern capturing something significant or interesting about the data and/or research question; there are no hard-and-fast rules — a theme is characterised by its significance. From the extract they grouped codes into six preliminary themes: The purpose of feedback Lecturers Reasons for using feedback (or not) How feedback is used (or not) Emotional response to feedback What students want from feedback. Most codes fit one theme; some fit more than one; a "miscellaneous" theme is allowed.

Phase 4 — reviewing themes: watch six themes become four

Review asks: Do the themes make sense? Does the data support them? Is too much crammed into one? Do overlapping themes really differ? Are there themes within themes (subthemes)? In the worked example the answers forced four concrete edits:

Phase 5 — define & name · the thematic map and the narrative

Defining means identifying the essence of each theme: What is the theme saying? How do subthemes interact? How do themes relate to each other? In the worked example What students want from feedback is the overarching theme, rooted in the other themes — students want feedback that both explains their grades and gives very specific guidance on improvement, and they see these as inextricably linked because improving understanding improves grades.

Findings writing uses verbatim quotes tagged with participant IDs and line numbers — e.g. "Actually if you had to tell me how I got a 60 or 67… What am I doing right in this piece of work?" (F1, lines 669–672), and "The referencing thing… that's really the only feedback we have gotten back… everything else it’s just kind of ‘well done’… hasn’t really told us much." (F1, lines 389–392). Verbal feedback is valued partly because a lecturer taking time to sit down "does mean a bit more" (M1, lines 456–458) — and the ideal was one-to-one in the lecturer's office, because feedback is potentially threatening and reactions are hard to manage in public.

The coding craft — the Bartindale–Varghese walkthrough distilled

Ten durable lessons from the walkthrough
  1. TA has a storytelling element — patterns become elements of the narrative you build; the hard work is telling the story well, not highlighting text.
  2. Read the transcript multiple times before coding anything; early ideas go to a separate notepad, not straight into codes.
  3. You always code through the lens of the research question — you are never a neutral blank page.
  4. You don't have to code every sentence (unless doing inductive line-by-line coding); skip what isn't interesting for your question.
  5. The obvious can be skipped: "mechanical engineers need technical skills" is true but not novel — it stood out to nobody.
  6. Codes evolve: rename a code and it suddenly covers more passages; split a code when you notice nuance; go back and revise earlier codes — it is an iterative, refining process.
  7. One passage can carry multiple codes (e.g. "gut feeling" + "making your best judgment with incomplete information").
  8. At coding time, don't worry about representativeness across transcripts — you can't know yet how important a code will become; clock what's important and refine later; drop codes that turn out uninteresting.
  9. Different researchers produce different codes — that is acceptable as long as every code is evidenced in the text, and it is acknowledged in the write-up (part of the story being told).
  10. Domain knowledge matters: things only "jump out" if you know enough about the area to see why they're interesting.

Phase 6 — producing the report (IxDF article)

The write-up must give the reader enough information about your project and process to evaluate the quality of your research — what you did, when, and how you analysed. Present each theme with quotes from participants to demonstrate findings; video, audio and photo evidence is even more convincing — but never without the participant's consent. And remember the humans behind the data: it is your job to make the participants feel real to the people you report your findings to.

Qualitative analysis has no single right answer: what must be right is the method — latent, multi-word, evidenced, iterative.

The ground rules (from the workshop slides — markers apply these)

  • Read the transcript at least twice before coding anything.
  • Codes must be as latent as possible — go beneath the surface, don't just restate what was said.
  • Never single- or double-word codes — one-word codes signal a bland, abstract description.
  • Reuse codes where the same underlying idea recurs (easier after a full pass — that refinement pass is iterative coding).
  • Thematic analysis goes beyond content analysis: content analysis would stay at descriptive/semantic codes; TA wants the underlying meaning.

Activity 1 · Fill in the blanks with latent codes

The template — the LADDER pattern for latent coding
  1. Listen to the surface claim (what the participant literally says).
  2. Ask what it reveals — about how they see the world, what they value, what problem they're managing. Why would someone say this?
  3. Draft the code as a multi-word conceptual phrase (3–8 words), not a summary noun.
  4. Dig for evidence: every word of the code must be traceable to the quoted text.
  5. Extract once, reuse later — if it recurs, the same code name applies; if a later passage adds nuance, split or rename (that's iteration, practised in Activity 2).
  6. Reject the merely true: skip statements that are obvious and uninformative for the research question (the "engineers need technical skills" test from the walkthrough).

Official calibration example (workshop slide): “I prefer written feedback to online feedback, as somehow I feel like the lecturer has spent time reading my essay” → semantic code: “preference for written feedback” · latent code: “written feedback adds a personal connection”. The latent rung answers why the preference exists.

Activity 2 · Coding practice — now iterate

The template — the ITERATE loop
  1. Code the whole transcript first with fresh eyes; don't polish mid-pass (the walkthrough: "at this stage, I'm just clocking anything that's important").
  2. Re-read your code list, not the transcript. Look for codes that say the same thing in different words → merge under one name.
  3. Look for codes that secretly carry two ideas → split them and revisit every earlier passage the old code touched, recoding as needed.
  4. Look for codes nothing important rides on → drop them without mercy.
  5. Rename for reach: a more precise name often turns out to cover passages you coded differently — go back and unify.
  6. Repeat passes until a full read changes nothing. That is iterative coding — coding is not a one-off activity.

Model answer A — the merge story. First pass on blocks 2–4 produced three codes: "saves time", "simplifies production", "efficient and practical". These are the same underlying idea wearing three surface outfits — all quote-supported ("saves time and simplifies production", "efficient and practical", "It's efficient"). Iteration pass: merge into one code, "adopting AI for production efficiency", and revisit the transcript so all three passages now carry the same label. Logic applied: ITERATE step 2 (merge near-duplicates). The payoff is thematic — one merged code with three supporting passages is exactly what a theme needs, whereas three scattered codes would have fragmented it.

Model answer B — the split story. First pass produced one code on the image sets: "AI images have accuracy issues". On re-reading, it secretly bundles two different failure mechanisms: physical plausibility flaws (unnaturally placed egg, too-perfect leaf) and contextual mismatch (background "gives it away as AI", table doesn't match the venue). Split into "detecting AI giveaways through physical implausibility" and "personalization must match the real-world context", then revisit block 2 and block 3 recoding each flaw to its mechanism. Logic applied: ITERATE step 3 (split a double idea) + step 6 (revisit earlier passages). This split matters for the findings: one mechanism is about AI technology limits, the other is about deployment fit — they belong to different themes.

Model answer C — the rename-for-reach story. First pass coded the 80%-accuracy remark as "quality is 80%" — a semantic dead end. Re-reading the surrounding lines ("the background gives it away as AI", "still need work, but these are manageable") shows the participant applying a tolerance threshold everywhere: flaws are noticed, weighed, and adopted past. Rename to "accepting near-enough quality as usable" and re-apply it to "Sample 3 is too bright" (chosen anyway? No — rejected)… careful: the rename must survive the evidence test, so the code covers only the passages where flaws are adopted despite being noted. Logic applied: ITERATE step 5 (rename for reach) disciplined by the LADDER evidence rung — renaming is not licence to over-claim.

Model answer D — the drop story. A first-pass code "interview is a follow-up" (from the interviewer's opening) describes the study setup, not the participant's meaning — it fails the research question's angle and adds no insight. Drop it. Logic applied: ITERATE step 4 (drop the unimportant) + the walkthrough's "you don't have to code every sentence". Interviewer's own turns are usually data only when they shape the participant's answers.

Activity 3 · Developing themes — from codes to a theme map and a rough abstract

The template — the THEME test (a code group earns theme status only if all five pass)
  1. Central organising concept — can you state in one sentence what holds the group together, that is more than the sum of its codes?
  2. Significance — does it say something important or interesting about the research question? (Braun & Clarke: a theme is characterised by its significance.)
  3. Internal coherence — do the codes genuinely belong together, or are you forcing two ideas into one box?
  4. External distinctness — does it overlap a neighbouring theme? If two themes share most of their codes, merge or subtheme them (Maguire & Delahunt merged "Reasons" + "How" into "Use of feedback").
  5. Evidence — is every code traceable to quoted text, with at least two illustrative quotes for the write-up?

Level 1 · Semantic or latent? — train your eye

For each quote, both code options are "correct" as descriptions — only one is latent. The pattern: a semantic code restates the surface content; a latent code names the underlying idea, assumption or value that makes the participant say it. Beyond the slides · the reliability concepts lecturers stretch into

Reflexivity

The walkthrough's closing point, formalised: because different researchers produce different evidenced codes, rigorous TA acknowledges the analyst's role — keep a reflexive journal of early impressions (the "separate notepad"), and report your position in the write-up. It converts a bias threat into an auditable decision trail.

Inter-coder reliability

Having two or more people code independently and then compare (the walkthrough's interviewer vs coder disagreements are mini-instances). Agreement is discussed, not just counted; the workshop's "comparing codes" activity is reliability work. Maguire & Delahunt: two coders are useful but not essential for credible TA.

Trustworthiness criteria

The qualitative answer to "is this research valid?" (Lincoln & Guba; Nowell et al. 2017): credibility (do the findings ring true to participants? — member checking), transferability (thick description lets readers judge fit), dependability (an auditable process — your code trail), confirmability (conclusions trace to data, not researcher bias).

Saturation

The stopping rule for data collection: you keep collecting until new data stops producing new codes/themes. It is the qualitative cousin of "enough sample" — and it's why "analyse as you collect" matters: you can't know you've saturated if analysis only starts after the last interview.

Stretch-quiz survival line: if a question mentions "two researchers coding the same transcript" → inter-coder reliability / triangulation of interpretation; "researcher's own assumptions" → reflexivity; "can readers trust this qualitative study" → trustworthiness (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability); "when to stop interviewing" → saturation.

Centre 1 · Mean (arithmetic average)

Add every value, divide by the count: x̄ = Σx / n. WPM: 1405 / 15 = 93.7 WPM. Age: 644 / 15 = 42.9 years. Strengths: uses every data point. Weaknesses: dragged by outliers and skew — a single extreme value can pull it far from where most data sits. Legal for interval/ratio data; meaningless for nominal categories.

Centre 2 · Median (middle value)

Sort, then take the middle (for odd n, position (n+1)/2; for even n, average the two middle values). WPM: 100. Age: 42. Robust to outliers — the classic exam move: "which average should you report for skewed data / data with an extreme value?" → median. Legal for ordinal and above.

Centre 3 · Mode (most frequent value)

WPM: 100 (three people). Age: 55 (twice). The only average legal for nominal data (most common faculty, most common colour). A dataset can be bimodal or have no mode; MATLAB returns ties via [~,~,C] = mode(data). Note how the WPM trio differ in what they tell us: mean 93.7 < median 100 — the slow tail (60–80) drags the mean down → left-skewed shape (long low tail).

Population vs sample — the most-marked distinction in this topic: population variance/SD divides by n; sample variance/SD divides by n−1. The bridge to inferential statistics (where Video 4 stops)

Descriptive → inferential in one paragraph.

Everything above describes the data you have. Inferential statistics asks the next question: what does this sample let us conclude about the population it came from? That requires hypothesis testing — state H₀ (nothing is going on), compute how surprising the data would be if H₀ were true (that's the p-value), and decide. The workshop's Activity 3 is your first full run: a two-sample t-test asking whether CS-degree holders type faster than non-holders. Weeks 5 goes deep on experimental design and hypothesis testing; week 4 gives you the vocabulary and the first hands-on execution.

Activity 3 · Inferential statistics — two-sample t-test, by hand then MATLAB

Data from the sheet: typing speeds (WPM) of 12 people without a CS degree vs 12 with a CS degree.

Without CS degree (n₁ = 12)

Σx = 960 → mean x̄₁ = 80.0
s₁² = 145.45 (s₁ = 12.06)

With CS degree (n₂ = 12)

85 · 95 · 100 · 90 · 105 · 105 · 100 · 110 · 85 · 70 · 110 · 85

The by-hand computation, every intermediate value
  1. Hypotheses (two-tailed): H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ (no difference in mean typing speed) · H₁: μ₁ ≠ μ₂.
  2. Pooled variance (equal-variances two-sample t): sp² = [(n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²] / (n₁+n₂−2) = [11×145.45 + 11×150.00] / 22 = (1600 + 1650) / 22 = 147.73.
  3. Standard error of the difference: SE = √(sp²·(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)) = √(147.73 × (1/12 + 1/12)) = √(147.73 / 6) = √24.62 = 4.962.
  4. t statistic: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SE = (80 − 95) / 4.962 = −15 / 4.962 = −3.02.
  5. Degrees of freedom: df = n₁ + n₂ − 2 = 22.
  6. Critical value from the sheet's t-table (two-tailed, α = 0.05): df = 22 isn't a row, so bracket it with df = 21 (2.080) and the printed z-row (∞, 1.960); interpolate mentally → ≈ 2.07.
  7. Decision: |t| = 3.02 > 2.07 → reject H₀ at the 5% level. In fact |t| also exceeds the α = 0.01 critical value (df 21 row: 2.831) → p < 0.01.
  8. Exact p-value (MATLAB): [h,p] = ttest2(noCS, withCS) → h = 1, p ≈ 0.0063 — broadly confirming the by-hand result, now exact.
Model answer — reporting it in one sentence (Findings style)

"An independent-samples t-test showed that typing speed differed significantly between CS-degree holders (M = 95.0 WPM, SD = 12.2) and non-holders (M = 80.0 WPM, SD = 12.1), t(22) = −3.02, p = .006, two-tailed — a 15 WPM advantage for the CS group." Pattern applied: statistic name → group descriptives → t with df → p → direction and size of the effect, in one breath. This exact sentence shape is what your assignment's Findings section wants.

Model answer — interpreting p for a sceptical reader

"If CS degrees had no effect, data this extreme (a 15-WPM gap or bigger) would occur only about 6 times in 1000 random samples — so either we got very unlucky, or the null is wrong. At α = 0.05 we reject the null." Pattern applied: p is the probability of data at least this extreme assuming H₀ is true — never "the probability H₀ is true". That inversion is the single most-marked mistake in this topic.

By-hand traps the tutors will plant: (1) dividing by n instead of n−1 when computing s² — inflates t slightly and changes the df logic; (2) using df = n₁+n₂ = 24 instead of 22; (3) reading the wrong tail — the sheet's table is two-tailed, so compare |t| directly; (4) forgetting that MATLAB's default ttest2 is two-tailed with pooled variance, matching the by-hand route; (5) reporting p < 0.05 as "p = 0.05".

Walkthrough 1 · Variance by hand — clean numbers first

The 5-step VIPS pattern (Variance In Plain Steps)
  1. Mean first. Every spread statistic starts from the centre. Teaching set: [60, 70, 90, 100, 120] → mean = 440/5 = 88.
  2. Deviations. Subtract the mean from every value: −28, −18, +2, +12, +32. Always check they sum to 0 — if not, the mean is wrong.
  3. Square them. 784, 324, 4, 144, 1024. Squaring converts distance into "area-like" magnitude and removes the sign problem (positive and negative deviations would cancel).
  4. Sum. 784+324+4+144+1024 = 2280.
  5. Divide — and here's the only decision in the whole formula: population (÷n = ÷5) → 2280/5 = 456; sample (÷(n−1) = ÷4) → 2280/4 = 570. Same sum, different story about what the data represents.

Now the real dataset: WPM (n = 15, mean 93.67) → Σ(x−x̄)² = 5173.33 → ÷15 = 344.89 (population) → ÷14 = 369.52 (sample). SD = √344.89 = 18.57 / √369.52 = 19.22. Notice: the sample version is always a bit larger — dividing by a smaller number has to be.

Walkthrough 2 · Reading the t-table without flinching

The 4-step TABLE pattern
  1. Tail first. The sheet's table is two-tailed — you'll compare |t| directly. (One-tailed tables halve the α columns; don't mix them.)
  2. df row. df = n₁ + n₂ − 2 for a pooled two-sample test. No exact row? Bracket with neighbours (df 21 → 2.080; df 24 → 2.064) and interpolate mentally — examiners accept the nearest conservative row.
  3. α column. 0.05 is the default decision line; the 0.01 / 0.001 columns let you report how strong the evidence is beyond "significant".
  4. Compare and conclude. |t| ≥ critical → reject H₀ at that α. If |t| falls between columns, report the bracket: "p < 0.05" or "0.01 < p < 0.02", never a made-up exact figure when reading by hand.

Worked on our test: df = 22, |t| = 3.02. df-21 row: 2.080 (α=.05), 2.831 (α=.01). 3.02 exceeds both → reject at 1% → p < 0.01; MATLAB's exact value: p = 0.0063 (consistent, since 0.0063 < 0.01).

Walkthrough 3 · p-values that survive interrogation

The correct reading (memorise the bold part):

p = 0.0063 means: if H₀ were true (no real difference), the probability of observing a gap this large or larger is 0.63%. It does not mean "the probability the null is true is 0.63%", and it does not mean "the result is 99.37% likely to replicate". Decision rule at α = 0.05: p < 0.05 → reject H₀. Strength language: p < .05 significant · p < .01 strong · p < .001 very strong. Also know the two error flavours for stretch questions: Type I = false positive (reject a true H₀ — happens with probability α) · Type II = false negative (keep a false H₀ — probability β). Lowering α trades one against the other.

Beyond the slides · the stretch concepts, one line each

  • Degrees of freedom — how many values are free to vary once the statistics are fixed; for two means, df = n₁+n₂−2. It parameterises the t-distribution's tail heaviness (small df → fat tails → bigger critical values).
  • Empirical rule (68–95–99.7) — for bell-shaped data: ≈68% within ±1 SD, ≈95% within ±2 SD, ≈99.7% within ±3 SD. A quick plausibility check for any reported mean/SD pair.
  • Five-number summary — min, Q1, median, Q3, max; the boxplot's skeleton and a robust companion to median + IQR.
  • Type I / Type II errors — false positive (α) vs false negative (β); the reason "p < .05" is a convention, not a law.
  • One- vs two-tailed tests — two-tailed: "different, direction unknown" (default, and what the sheet's table assumes); one-tailed: "specifically greater/less", decided before seeing data. Switching to one-tailed after peeking is p-hacking.
  • Effect size — p says "is there a difference"; effect size says "how big". Our t-test: a 15-WPM gap on ~12-WPM SDs is a large, practical difference — worth one clause in Findings even at this small n.

WEEK 4 Intuition lab — reason your way to every rule

This section is deliberately different: instead of telling you the rules, it lets you reach them yourself. Each trail starts from a small question with tiny numbers you can check in your head. Cover the revealed answer, commit to your own, then compare. If your reasoning and the trail converge, the rule is yours — quiz-proof.

How to use the trails

  • 1 · Cover. Read the setup only. The answer is hidden in a fold.
  • 2 · Predict. Write (or say) your answer and a reason before opening the fold.
  • 3 · Verify. Open the fold — check the arithmetic yourself, don't trust it.
  • 4 · Conclude. The "So the rule is…" line is the part worth remembering; everything else was scaffolding.

Trail 1 · Why variance squares the deviations — the dead end you must discover first

Suppose you want "average distance from the mean" and you reach for it honestly: take the teaching set [60, 70, 90, 100, 120], mean 88. The deviations are −28, −18, +2, +12, +32.

Predict: add the deviations. What do you get — and, more importantly, what would you get for any dataset?

Open when you've committed to an answer

−28 − 18 + 2 + 12 + 32 = 0. And it can never be anything else: the mean is defined as the point where the distances above it exactly cancel the distances below it — Σ(x − x̄) = Σx − n·x̄ = n·x̄ − n·x̄ = 0. A spread measure that is always zero is a dead end.

So negative signs must go. Two roads: (a) take absolute values |d|, or (b) square them d². Both kill the cancellation. Squaring wins in practice because it is smooth, easy to combine across formulas (variances add for independent quantities — used in the t-test's standard error), and it deliberately punishes large deviations — which is usually what you want a spread measure to notice. The cost: squared units (WPM²), which is exactly why the standard deviation exists — a square-root "undo" that returns to WPM.

So the rule is: variance = mean of squared deviations, not mean of deviations; and SD = √variance exists purely to fix the units. The squaring isn't a mystery convention — it's the simplest escape from a cancellation that is guaranteed by the definition of the mean.

Trail 2 · Why the sample variance divides by n−1 — follow the bias to its source

Take the smallest possible sample: [10, 20], sample mean 15. Deviations: −5, +5. Squared: 25, 25. Sum: 50.

Predict: if these two values were a sample from a much larger population, which divisor — n = 2 or n−1 = 1 — would give a variance closer to the population's true spread? To decide, ask a sharper question first: is the sample mean (15) closer to the data or is the true population mean closer?

Open when you've committed to an answer

The sample mean is the single closest point to the data — it minimises the sum of squared deviations by definition (move the fulcrum anywhere else and the total squared distance grows). The true population mean is virtually never exactly the sample mean, so measured from the sample mean, the data always looks tighter than it really is. The spread is systematically under-estimated.

How big is the bias? Averaged over all possible samples, dividing by n returns (n−1)/n of the true variance — for n = 2, exactly half. That's the number you found above: 50/2 = 25 is the biased (÷n) estimate, while 50/1 = 50 is the corrected (÷n−1) one. Dividing by n−1 inflates the estimate by exactly n/(n−1), cancelling the deflation — Bessel's correction.

So the rule is: ÷n describes the data you have (population); ÷(n−1) repairs the under-estimate when the data is a sample of something bigger. The whole distinction is one sentence long once you've seen the bias: the sample mean sits too close to the sample.

Trail 3 · Mean vs median — two different questions wearing similar clothes

Look at [1, 2, 3, 4, 100].

Predict: the mean is ___, the median is ___. Now answer in words: (a) "the point where the data balances" — which statistic is that? (b) "the value with half the data points on each side, ignoring how far they are" — which statistic is that?

Open when you've committed

Mean = 110/5 = 22. Median = 3. The balance point (a) is the mean — picture the numbers as weights on a ruler: the 100 is a heavy far-out weight, so the fulcrum slides toward it. The halfway-by-count point (b) is the median — it only counts how many values sit on each side, never how heavy or far.

Everything else about these two statistics falls out of that single difference. A far-out value changes distances enormously (dragging the balance point) but changes counts almost not at all — here the median moved from 2.5 (for [1,2,3,4]) to 3, while the mean jumped from 2.5 to 22. That is why the median is robust. And the skew rule is the same fact seen from the other side: a long tail means extreme values on one side; the mean is pulled toward them, the median isn't; so the mean sits on the tail's side of the median — mean < median ⟹ low tail ⟹ left-skew (the WPM data: 93.67 below 100).

So the rule is: mean = balance point (cares about distance, outlier-sensitive); median = halfway-by-count (cares about order, robust). Report the median when distance-based answers would be hostage to extreme values.

Trail 4 · Why measurement levels gate the statistics — ask what each statistic *does*

Don't memorise a permissions table. Instead, take each statistic and ask: what operation does it secretly perform on the data?

The mean subtracts. "Balance point" means adding distances on both sides and comparing — and subtracting only makes sense if the gaps are comparable. On a Likert scale, is the distance from 1→2 really the same as 4→5? Nobody guarantees it. On nominal labels (faculty names) there is no distance at all. So the mean quietly demands interval-or-better data.

The median only sorts. It asks "who is in the middle?" — pure ordering. Anything with a rank order (Likert, frequency bands, education levels) supports it. The mode only matches. It asks "which value repeats most?" — pure identity counting. Even colours and faculty names support that.

So the rule builds itself: mode ← identity (any data) · median ← order (ordinal+) · mean ← distance (interval/ratio+). When a quiz asks "which statistic is appropriate for…", translate the data into what operations it permits and the answer is forced.

Trail 5 · Why the t-test looks the way it does — signal over noise, built from variances

The workshop formula t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SE has a shape shared by every test statistic you'll meet: something observed ÷ how much of it you'd expect by chance. You can derive the denominator yourself from one fact you already own: variances add for independent quantities.

Build it: each group mean wobbles from sample to sample — Var(mean of n values) = σ²/n (averaging shrinks the wobble by the group size; this is why bigger samples are more trustworthy). The two groups are independent, so Var(x̄₁ − x̄₂) = σ²/n₁ + σ²/n₂. Take the square root and that's the standard error: the typical size of the group difference even when nothing is going on. Our numbers: √(147.73·(1/12+1/12)) = 4.96 WPM of pure chance.

Now read the t value as a sentence: t = −3.02 says the observed gap (15 WPM) is about 3 chance-units big. A gap of 1 SE happens all the time by luck; 3 SEs is rare — the t-table tells you exactly how rare (p ≈ .006). The pooled variance sp² is just the best combined estimate of the σ² both groups share, weighting each group's estimate by its n−1 (more data → more say). And df = n₁+n₂−2 counts the independent squared deviations that built that estimate.

So the rule is: t = signal ÷ noise. Learn to read every test statistic this way — z, t, χ², F — and they stop being formulas and start being measurements.

Trail 6 · What a p-value actually is — construct the "null world" and walk into it

The most-marked error in this topic is an inversion: "p = .006 means the null is 0.6% likely." You can see for yourself why that's wrong by building the world the p-value lives in.

Step 1. Assume H₀ hard: there is no real difference between the groups. In this world, the 15-WPM gap we saw can only be random allocation noise — two samples from the same population landing apart by chance.

Step 2. Ask: in this null world, how often would randomness produce a gap at least this big? The t-distribution (df 22) answers: the tails beyond ±3.02 cover 0.0063 of all possible outcomes. That is the p-value.

Step 3. Now the inversion is visible: p was computed inside the null world — it is P(data this extreme | H₀). The wrong reading, P(H₀ | data), lives in a different world (it requires prior probabilities, which you don't have). Same two ingredients, flipped condition — that's the entire difference between a correct and a fatal interpretation.

So the rule is: p = "how often the null world produces this", not "how likely the null is". Decision logic: 0.0063 is rarer than the 1-in-20 rarity we agreed (α = .05) to tolerate — so we call it: reject H₀. A 0.6%-lucky day is possible; we just don't bet the study on it.

Trail 7 · Why themes must come from codes — a thought experiment that settles it

Someone proposes: "let's save time — use the interview questions as the themes."

Test the proposal with one filter: could a person produce that output without ever reading a single transcript?

Open when you've committed

Yes, trivially — anyone holding the interview schedule could write "Theme 1: experiences with medication, Theme 2: daily routines…" without reading a word anyone said. Now the conclusion is forced: any output that can be produced without the data cannot be analysis of the data. Analysis must be something the transcripts make possible that the schedule alone doesn't.

That's exactly what codes add: they are built from the participants' own words, so the themes built from them carry information no schedule can contain — the difference between what you planned to ask and what people actually told you. This is Clarke & Braun's pitfall in one line, derived rather than quoted: question-themes are summarising; code-themes are analysing.

So the rule is: whatever your analysis produces must be impossible to produce without the data. It's a clean audit you can run on any claimed theme, code, or finding.

Trail 8 · Semantic vs latent — the "ask why" drill, done once properly

Take the transcript line: "It's free to enter, and families tend to come in groups, which increases revenue." Now apply one mechanical rule: ask WHY the participant said it, and keep asking until the answer no longer appears in the sentence.

Predict: what is the participant's reason for mentioning this? Is that reason stated in the sentence?

Open when you've committed

The sentence states facts (free entry, groups, revenue) but not why it's worth saying. Push: why mention that free entry raises revenue? Because the participant reads group behaviour as a revenue mechanism — the café's design converts accessibility into income. That claim is nowhere in the words; it is the thinking behind the words. That is the latent content, and the code it earns ("using group visits to support revenue generation") is the workshop's own example.

The semantic code ("the café is free to enter") stops one question too early — it reports the surface and never reaches the participant's reasoning. So the distinction isn't vague or taste-based: semantic = the answer while you can still point at the sentence; latent = the answer only when you can't. The "ask why" drill is the tool that tells you which level you've reached.

So the rule is: keep asking why until the reason isn't in the sentence anymore — that's where latent codes live.

Sanity checks that catch wrong answers before you commit

  • Deviations must sum to 0. If your computed mean doesn't make them, the mean is wrong. Free check on any hand calculation.
  • Variance ≥ 0, always. It's a mean of squares. A negative "variance" is an arithmetic error, full stop.
  • SD = 0 means every value identical. No spread, no deviation — a good extreme-case test of understanding.
  • |t| near 0 → data matches the null; |t| large → data fights the null. The whole test is one ratio; if your t came out 0.3, p will be large and you will not reject — check your sign and arithmetic before concluding anything stranger.
  • df is never n₁+n₂. The −2 is the price of estimating two group means. If you forgot it, every critical value you looked up is slightly too small.
  • A code you can't point to in the transcript is a hypothesis, not a code. The evidence test doubles as a sanity check: no anchor, no code.
  • Means of small integers should look "too clean" if wrong. Mean 93.67 vs 93.0: if your by-hand mean of the WPM column is a round number, you dropped or double-counted a row (there are 15).
