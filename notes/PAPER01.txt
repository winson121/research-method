Development and validation of open-source deep neural 
networks for comprehensive chest x-ray reading: 
a retrospective, multicentre study
Yashin Dicente Cid*, Matthew Macpherson*, Louise Gervais-Andre, Yuanyi Zhu, Giuseppe Franco, Ruggiero Santeramo, Chee Lim, Ian Selby, 
Keerthini Muthuswamy, Ashik Amlani, Heath Hopewell, Das Indrajeet, Maria Liakata, Charles E Hutchinson, Vicky Goh, Giovanni Montana
Summary
Background Artificial intelligence (AI) systems for automated chest x-ray interpretation hold promise for standardising 
reporting and reducing delays in health systems with shortages of trained radiologists. Yet, there are few freely 
accessible AI systems trained on large datasets for practitioners to use with their own data with a view to accelerating 
clinical deployment of AI systems in radiology. We aimed to contribute an AI system for comprehensive chest x-ray 
abnormality detection.
Methods In this retrospective cohort study, we developed open-source neural networks, X-Raydar and X-Raydar-
NLP, for classifying common chest x-ray findings from images and their free-text reports. Our networks were 
developed using data from six UK hospitals from three National Health Service (NHS) Trusts (University Hospitals 
Coventry and Warwickshire NHS Trust, University Hospitals Birmingham NHS Foundation Trust, and University 
Hospitals Leicester NHS Trust) collectively contributing 2  513 546 chest x-ray studies taken from a 13-year period 
(2006–19), which yielded 1  940 508 usable free-text  radiological reports written by the contemporary assessing 
radiologist (collectively referred to as the “historic reporters”) and 1  896 034 frontal images. Chest x-rays were 
labelled using a taxonomy of 37 findings by a custom-trained natural language processing (NLP) algorithm, 
X-Raydar-NLP, from the original free-text reports. X-Raydar-NLP was trained on 23  230 manually annotated reports 
and tested on 4551 reports from all hospitals. 1  694 921 labelled images from the training set and 89  238 from the 
validation set were then used to train a multi-label image classifier. Our algorithms we re evaluated on three 
retrospective datasets: a set of exams sampled randomly from the full NHS dataset reported during clinical practice 
and annotated using NLP (n=103  328); a consensus set sampled from all six hospitals annotated by three  expert 
radiologists (two independent annotators for each image and a third consultant to facilitate disagreement resolution) 
under research conditions (n=1427); and an independent dataset, MIMIC-CXR, consisting of NLP-annotated exams 
(n=252 374).
Findings X-Raydar achieved a mean AUC of 0·919 (SD 0·039) on the auto-labelled set, 0·864 (0·102) on the consensus 
set, and 0·842 (0·074) on the MIMIC-CXR test, demonstrating similar performance to the historic clinical radiologist 
reporters, as assessed on the consensus set, for multiple clinically important findings, including pneumothorax, 
parenchymal opacification, and parenchymal mass or nodules. On the consensus set, X-Raydar outperformed 
historical reporter balanced accuracy with significance on 27 of 37 findings, was non-inferior on nine, and inferior on 
one finding, resulting in an average improvement of 13·3% (SD 13∙1) to 0∙763 (0·110), including a mean 5∙6% (13·2) 
improvement in critical findings to 0∙826 (0·119).
Interpretation Our study shows that automated classification of chest x-rays under a comprehensive taxonomy can 
achieve performance levels similar to those of historical reporters and exhibit robust generalisation to external data. 
The open-sourced neural networks can serve as foundation models for further research and are freely available to the 
research community.

Introduction
The chest x-ray accounts for 40% of the 3 ·6 billion 
imaging procedures performed worldwide each year1 as a 
result of its efficacy in diagnosing cardiopulmonary 
abnormalities, including those related to COVID-19, 2 
coupled with a relatively low cost, low radiation burden, 
and ease of acquisition. In 2021, WHO also recommended 
strategic screening of tuberculosis, one of the top 
infectious killers worldwide, with more than 10 million 
cases each year, through chest x-ray screening of at-risk 
populations.
3 Given the volume of chest x-ray 
examinations and potential human resource barrier to

encountered in clinical practice is preferable. Easy 
accessibility to such algorithms by non-AI specialists 
remains relatively low. Although several commercial 
clinical platforms are now available, to the best of our 
knowledge the only extant non-commercial web-based 
platform for real-time chest x-ray reading is Chester,

publicly accessible resources, such as a recent foundation 
model providing feature embeddings
15 and a standardised 
library of models, 16 are aimed more at the AI research 
specialist community.
We aimed to contribute an AI system for comprehensive 
chest x-ray abnormality detection, trained on a novel 
UK-sourced chest x-ray dataset. We assessed its perfor -
mance in comparison to historical reporters working in 
real-world, time-constrained clinical conditions.
Methods 
Workflow 
In this retrospective study, we first developed a custom 
NLP algorithm (X-Raydar-NLP) to automatically extract 
comprehensive binary labels from the free-text reports 
for each image using a 37-finding radiological taxonomy 
(figure 1). The labelled images were then used to train 
and test our computer vision algorithm, X-Raydar, in a 
fully supervised machine-learning setting. Model 
development and analysis were performed in the Nvidia 
PyTorch (version 21.02) Docker container environ  ment 
(Ubuntu [version 20.04], PyTorch [version 1.8], and 
Python [version 3.8]) with pydicom (version 2.1) and 
Grassroots Digital Imaging and Communications in 
Medicine (DICOM; version 2.8.9), with Scikit-learn 
(version 1.0.2) used in results analysis.

Foundation Trust (Queen Elizabeth Hospital

Research in context
Evidence before this study
A search of PubMed over the 10 years preceding Oct 1, 2022, 
with the keywords “chest” AND [“x-ray” OR “CXR” OR “xray”] 
AND [“automat*” OR “classification”] AND [“deep learning” OR 
“machine learning”] yielded 960 papers after removing non 
x-ray modality studies. Of these papers, 599 were specific to 
COVID-19 classification, 202 focused on other single-finding, 
proof-of-concept work, 114 addressed segmentation or 
technical computer vision aspects, and 11 were review papers. 
The remaining papers predominantly addressed multi-label 
classification under the US National Institutes of Health ChestX-
ray14 ontology using Chest14, Chexpert, and Medical 
Information Mart for Intensive Care public datasets for training 
and assessment. Two studies were identified that attempted 
comprehensive ontological coverage, and only one work was 
found that offered public non-commercial web-based 
assessment.
Added value of this study
This study is based on what is, to the best of our knowledge, the 
largest chest x-ray dataset in the UK, comprising scans from all 
clinical settings across six hospitals. The data cover a diverse 
population of 4 million people over a period of 13 years, 
providing a valuable alternative to existing studies using data 
from other populations. It is one of the few studies to tackle 
comprehensive multi-label chest X-ray classification, using a 
37-finding taxonomy balancing clinical detail with parsimony 
suited to artificial intelligence training. We compare model 
performance against historical real-world clinical reporting, 
showing that the model outperforms the historically observed 
performance of radiologists in real, time-constrained clinical 
practice. We provide access to our trained computer vision and 
natural language processing neural networks via open-source 
code with pre-trained weights, a user-friendly web portal, and 
programmable application programming interfaces for image 
and report labelling.
Implications of all the available evidence
This study, along with other recent work, indicates that 
automated chest x-ray interpretation at close to human expert 
level is now a reality and that models trained on large datasets 
can overcome generalisation concerns, which have held back 
deployment. By making our model freely available, we hope to 
accelerate the adoption of automated systems in clinical 
settings to help relieve acute pressures on health-care systems 
in the post-COVID-19 environment.

Taxonomy 
To label the acquired images, a team of three senior 
radiologists (VG, CEH, and LG-A) designed a radiological 
taxonomy with the dual objectives of completeness (ie, 
full coverage of the reported findings in our data) and 
parsimony. The resulting taxonomy covers eight 
anatomical areas (lung, mediastinum and hila; pleural 
space; diaphragm; cardiovascular; osseus structures and 
chest wall; skin and soft tissue; and abdomen) and non-
anatomical structures (eg, medical object and foreign 
body). For each area, a minimal set of descriptive labels 
was developed to encompass key radiological findings. 
Findings were also grouped by clinical importance, 
referred to as criticality, with three levels: critical, urgent, 
and non-urgent. In total, the taxonomy comprised 
45 categories: 37 radiological findings and eight report-
specific or technical categories (table 1).

Computer vision algorithm 
For the development of our computer vision algorithm, 
X-Raydar, the abnormality detection task was framed as a 
multi-label image classification (MLC) problem: given a 
frontal chest x-ray (two-dimensional) image, we developed 
an algorithm able to return the probability that each one 
of our 37 clinical findings (labels) was detected on the 
image. An implementation of InceptionV3
18 was used as 
our feature extraction convolutional neural network given 
its proven potential to produce state-of-the-art results for 
the multi-label classification of chest x-rays.
19 Comparing 
various approaches to the MLC problem, we found the 
strongest results from a prediction algorithm trained 
using a custom loss function incorporating label 
uncertainties derived from the NLP model and class 
weighting factors derived from prevalences observed in 
the training data. X-Raydar takes the mean probability of 
each finding across three versions of the model trained 
at 299², 512², and 1024² resolutions, which we found to 
give improved performance versus any individual 
resolution. Full details of the algorithms, alternatives 
explored, and training process are given in the appendix 
(pp 8–11), along with a study of the effects of training set 
size and label noise on performance (appendix pp 12–13) 
and a study of model interpretability using saliency 
heatmaps (appendix pp 14–15). Performance of X-Raydar 
was also compared with a range of public models on the 
TorchXRayVision
16 library using the MIMIC-CXR dataset.

by manual annotation of the selected reports. Using 
AnnotateX, the radiologists were asked to label each

rectangular bounding box. The images were presented in

and displayed using a DICOM library that gave the

Each image was annotated in a research environment, 
with no time constraints and without any access to 
radiological reports, clinical history, or any other patient 
information. The radiologists, working independently, 
were asked to identify any finding present in our 
taxonomy, regardless of the finding’s criticality. When 
findings had disagreement in presence or location 
between the two annotators, the annotators were first 
asked to reconfirm their annotation; remaining 
disagreements (736 [9 ·5%] of 7774 total findings across 
images) reviewed by the two original annotators 
alongside a third senior radiologist (VG), resulting in a 
consensus agreement for all cases (appendix pp 7–8). 
Including the review process, each radiologist spent on 
average 14·75 min per image. The total development 
time took approximately 8 weeks per person on a full-
time basis.
Two larger NLP-labeled datasets were also used for 
testing. The first, called the auto-labelled set (n=103 328), 
was developed by randomly sampling our full dataset so 
as to capture a considerably larger number of cases per 
finding and mirror the dataset prevalences and patient 
demographics more closely. We present a full comparison 
of the auto-labelled characteristics versus our full dataset 
in the appendix (pp 7, 32−33), demon  strating that this 
size of test set is representative of our population. The 
training data, auto-labelled, and consensus test sets were 
constructed to be disjoint with respect to the anonymised 
patient identifiers provided to us by the participating 
hospital networks to ensure cross-contamination of 
patients between datasets did not occur.
The second NLP-labelled test set was an external 
validation dataset, MIMIC-CXR,
20 motivated by the large 
number of studies in this database as well as the 
availability of raw DICOM files (as opposed to pre-
processed images) with associated raw free-text reports 
(as opposed to pre-processed labels using a pre-existing 
taxonomy). This allowed a validation study under the 
exact same experimental conditions (exclusion criteria, 
data pre-processing and radiological taxonomy). This 
dataset consists of x-rays acquired at the Beth Israel 
Deaconess Medical Center Emergency Department 
during the period 2011–16. Starting from an initial pool 
of 377  110 images, our exclusion and pre-processing 
pipeline resulted in 252  374 exams. X-Raydar -NLP was 
used (without fine tuning) to automatically label the free-
text reports under our taxonomy.
Development of open-source and web-based tools for 
free public access
We deployed our model and DICOM pre-processing 
pipeline in a web portal open to public access. The 
website is designed for a general audience, allowing 
automatic pre-processing and real-time online classi -
fication of user-uploaded DICOM images (appendix 
p 29), along with application programming interfaces 
(APIs) for X-Raydar and X-Raydar-NLP. For the specialist 
AI community, our trained network architectures are 
freely available for download for research purposes, 
providing to the community an alternative foundation 
model based on a broad sampling of the UK population, 
which has hitherto been poorly represented in the 
literature.

Discussion 
We developed and tested a deep learning system capable 
of interpreting frontal chest x-rays with recognition of 
37 radiological findings spanning routine radiological 
practice, achieving a mean AUC of 0· 864 across our 
taxonomy and 0· 943 on critical findings as compared 
with expert-annotated consensus ground truth labels on 
1427 images. Normal scans are identified with an AUC 
of 0·941, and establishing appropriate model thresholds 
per finding to filter normal scans with high specificity 
would be an interesting topic for further work. 
Performance on the NLP-labelled test set was stronger 
with mean AUC 0∙919 (SD 0∙019) across all findings, 
which is unsurprising given that the labels were obtained 
using the same methodology as for X-Raydar’s training 
data, whereas the consensus set were annotated under 
more stringent conditions. Using the consensus set 
labels as a comprehensive ground truth, we were able to  
directly compare the performance of the historical 
reporters to X-Raydar. At a fixed model threshold of 0∙50, 
X-Raydar achieved a balanced accuracy of 0∙763 versus 
0∙678 for the historic reports; selecting model thresholds 
to match the observed human specificity levels, X-Raydar 
gave statistically superior performance in nine findings 
and inferior in two. These results and our analysis of 
historical under-reporting patterns (appendix p 8) indicate 
that X-Raydar could potentially improve the accuracy and 
comprehensiveness of reporting in the health service 
studied in both triage and second-reader roles.
There has been substantial interest in chest x-ray AI 
systems globally as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic 
and since the recommendations from the WHO with 
respect to tuberculosis screening. Acknowledging the 
need to improve non-AI specialists’ access to such 
systems, we have aimed to open source our work entirely 
to assist future deployment of similar AI tools. X-Raydar 
can be accessed directly through X-Raydar online, a user-
friendly web interface for a general audience that 
facilitates the process of uploading multiple DICOM 
files, pre-processing the images, and displaying the 
results in-browser (eg, see appendix p 29). This service is 
run on a secure server hosted at the University of 
Warwick (Warwick, UK) and is also accessible via a 
programmable API. For the research community, we 
take the unusual step of making our full pre-trained 
network weights available under an academic use license, 
allowing our models to be used in a full foundation role 
for transfer learning and fine tuning to alternative 
ontologies, datasets, and reporting tasks.
Direct comparisons with similar studies are generally 
challenging in the field due to the absence of a 
standardised taxonomy, variations in clinical settings 
and demographics of the datasets used, and variation in 
assessment protocols. The majority of work in the field 
also considers either single finding detection
21 or multi-
label classification within a partial taxonomy 11,13 as 
opposed to comprehensive classification. However, we 
have identified two studies assessing the performance of 
AI systems attempting comprehensive classification 
versus radiologists in a research setting for comparison. 
Wu and colleagues
22 adopted a 72-finding taxonomy and 
trained their model with the MIMIC 20 and US National 
Institutes of Health 23 datasets with automated label 
extraction, reporting a mean AUC of 0 ·807 across 
findings and outperforming third-year radiology 
residents in a research setting. Seah and colleagues
24 
trained a model with more than 800  000 images from 
various public datasets, hand labelled by 120 consultant 
radiologists under a 127-finding taxonomy, reporting a 
mean AUC of 0· 957 and improving radiologists’ 
labelling performance when available in a research 
setting. Our methodology is most directly comparable to 
that of Wu and colleagues
22 given the shared use of auto-
labelled training data from historical reports, with our 
model showing a higher headline performance at least 
partially attributable to the larger training set available to 
us. We attribute the performance gap with Seah and 
colleagues to the expert labelling of training data under a 
defined taxonomy; although we have shown strong 
performance in the more scalable and resource-light 
paradigm of NLP label extraction and demonstrated the 
robustness of training to the associated label noise 
(appendix pp 12–13), it is likely that the performance on 
our dataset could be materially improved with full 
manual annotation, if resources were available for us to 
do so.

Generalisability is a major concern in the field, with 
most published work necessarily relying on one or more 
of a relatively small range of pre-labelled public 
datasets
11,20,23,25,26 often representing single hospitals or 
clinical settings (eg, emergency medicine) because of the 
practical and legal difficulties of gathering new data, and 
thus depend on a limited range of demographics, 
hospitals, clinical settings, and imaging hardware, 
raising questions over cross-domain adaptability.
27,28 
Studies using novel, diverse datasets are therefore of 
interest to the community—eg, a recently available 
foundation model making feature embeddings available 
for downstream use from a CNN trained on a novel 
dataset of more than 800  000 images from India and the 
USA14—albeit without offering access to network weights. 
Progress towards improving trans  parency and stan -
dardisation is also being made, such as the recent 
establishment of a Python library of standardised 
datasets and pre-trained models
15 that aims to improve 
reproducibility standards in the field. Much care was 
therefore taken in the development of our DICOM 
preprocessing module so that any potential scanner-
related effects could be removed (appendix pp 2–3). Our 
experiments on the generalisation perfor  mance of our 
model on the MIMIC-CXR dataset 19 with preprocessing 
through our pipeline showed similar performance levels 
to our consensus test set without any fine-tuning, despite 
an entirely separate demographic, clinical setting (ie, 
emergency room) and differences in prevalence of 
findings from our UK-sourced data. This is promising 
evidence towards the goal of developing a single deep-
learning system that could be applied in health systems 
globally with confidence and supports our proposed use 
case as an alternative foundation model for adaptation to 
other reporting tasks. Investigating other (eg, sub-
Saharan) populations is an important  topic for further 
work; we hope that our open-source resources will 
facilitate these comparisons.
In our study, we aimed to use historical reporter 
performance as a proxy for the realistic level of detail that 
a reader is likely to give in a time-constrained and 
resource-constrained real-world clinical setting and 
evaluate how an AI algorithm compares with this 
performance and could provide benefits to a health 
service. Although we do not aim to prove that X-Raydar 
can exceed human performance in an unconstrained 
laboratory test setting (which is approximated by the 
consensus set as a gold standard), our results provide 
strong evidence that it can be of practical use in both a 
second-reading capacity for critical findings and to 
produce more comprehensive reports than are currently 
possible under realistic conditions by addressing sources 
of under-reporting.
We presented a comparison with the historical 
performance of all reporting radiologists, showing AI 
outperformed them in the majority of findings. The 
resource-intensive manual annotation process limited the 
number of images that could be included in the consensus 
ground truth set, leading to small sample sizes in some 
findings; for this reason, our results are supported by an 
additional large NLP-labelled test set. Although our study 
has the advantage of evaluating automated reporting 
against the demonstrated level of accuracy in a working 
clinical setting, it is not possible to definitively assess 
where a reporter might have deliberately under-reported 
secondary or irrelevant findings, either by focusing on the 
clinical question posed (which probably accounts for the 
increased performance gap vs the AI for less critical 
findings) or through human error or time constraints; our 
extensive analysis (appendix p 8)  of under-reporting 
patterns in the ground truth set indicate that both play a 
part. Our study does not consider the potential for model 
bias due to patient ethnicity, sex, or socioeconomic status, 
factors, as some data were not available (eg, ethnicity and 
socioeconomic status) that should be investigated before 
any use of the deep-learning system in clinical settings. 
We also consider only radiologists working within three 
hospital networks in the UK health service, which might 
not be representative of the reporting standards and 
workflow in other health services.
We have developed and tested a multi-label classifier 
chest x-ray AI system with a comprehensive taxonomy 
designed to balance clinical utility with suitability for NLP 
label extraction. We have confirmed model performance 
against a public dataset and real-world clinical reporting. 
There are few freely accessible AI systems for practitioners 
to use with their own data. We make our model available 
via a user-friendly web portal, allowing DICOM images to 
be uploaded without technical barriers, with the aim of 
facilitating clinical imple  mentation of AI in resource-
poor health-care systems. We make our full pre-trained 
model weights for both X-Raydar and X-Raydar-NLP 
available to the research community for evaluation and 
use as an alternative foundation model.

Data sharing 
X-Raydar is available for online use via an online portal x-raydar.info 
hosted on a secure University of Warwick server, along with APIs for both 
X-Raydar-CV and X-Raydar-NLP. Models, network weights and code are 
also available to researchers for non-commercial purposes at 
www.x-raydar.info and https://github.com/x-raydar/. The original images, 
free-text reports, and other patient data received from the NHS trusts 
cannot be shared currently due to confidentiality agreements in place with 
the contributing hospital networks.
