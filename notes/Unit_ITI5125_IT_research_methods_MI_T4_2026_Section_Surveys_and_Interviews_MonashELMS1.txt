 Overview
            

Surveys and Interviews

This week, we will explore how to
 conduct a proper survey as one way of collecting data or information. We will also learn how to 
design interview and analyse the data from interview (or other qualitative data like focus group discussion transcript) by using qualitative analysis. Finally, we will learn how to identify the types of interviews and focus group discussion that can be arranged and discuss its good and bad practices.

Week's Learning Outcomes:

Understand the purpose and role of questionnaires in the data collection process.

Identify and describe the different types of questionnaire questions that can be deployed.

Design a questionnaire to capture specific information from a cohort using best practice.

Understand the possible tools available for deploying surveys and questionnaires.

Understand how interviews and focus groups can be used to capture qualitative information.

Identify the types of interviews and focus groups that can be arranged.

Critique interviews for good and bad practice.

Be able to plan an interview appropriate for a topic.

Describe the characteristics of interviews and focus groups in the context of research.

Introduction

It���s our first week looking at qualitative methods ��� these are simple methods focussed around (usually) non-numerical data which is obtained through methods which engage people (users) often in subjective terms. 
One of the most common and easy to deploy qualitative methods is the survey or questionnaire. These are great for collecting large data samples without direct contact with a participant (such as online). Surveys must be carefully designed however as there are a number of easy pitfalls which can invalidate your results or make them harder to interpret.
No doubt you will have come across surveys regularly in your online life ��� they are often deployed when an organisation is asking a research question about a users��� experience of their service or system (such as the SETU). As you are aware, in the example of the SETU, there are many different types of questions that can be asked (and data that can be obtained) from a survey.
 

Creating and deploying a survey:
 Surveys consisting of multiple questions that a participant fills in on their own are a great way of capturing qualitative data at scale, but need careful design to obtain data that can be interpreted and used to answer a research question.

Case Study

Daniela is a data scientist at an IT consultancy commissioned by the government to design a digital platform for public health communications related to COVID-19. The government is hoping to use this platform to make their public health communications (for non-pharmaceutical interventions) more targeted and effective. However, the lack of current data and the novelty of the situation make it difficult for the team to make any informed decisions about how to design the platform, as not enough data exists to draw any conclusions on whether people have changed their behaviour in response to communications during the pandemic.
Due the rapid-changing nature of the pandemic, Daniela and team realise the importance of timely data collection. To do this, the team decided to conduct a large-scale survey through Facebook, using the Facebook Insights tool in order to reach the largest possible number of people across different states and territories. The team would use the Facebook Ads Manager to create advertisement campaigns to recruit respondents for their survey. However, they understand that respondents recruited randomly through Facebook may not necessarily be representative of the wider population, so they would start the survey with a ���Demographic Data��� section where information such as age, sex and education is captured. This section is crucial for them in order to gain an understanding of specific trends for each segment of the population. With the fast-paced nature of pandemic, the team does not have a lot of time to design the survey. However, based on their extensive experience with surveys, they realise that good survey design is crucial for collecting reliable data that would give them valid insights.
To reflect this, they identified 4 main topics that they wanted their survey to cover: relevant underlying medical conditions, adoption of preventive behaviours, perception of how the public health system has responded, and awareness of current public health advice. When designing survey questions, they would make sure that the survey was not lengthy, and would try to keep it to under 10 minutes to fill out. This would involve prioritising the use of closed-ended, multiple choice questions over open-ended questions. Questions around the respondents��� perceptions of the effectiveness of the public health system would be formulated using the Likert scales to capture nuance in various dimensions and simplify the statistical analysis of responses. For example, in relation to the topic of preventive behaviours, they would ask respondents to report which measures, if any, they had taken to protect themselves from COVID-19. For this question, they would provide multiple choice answers based on preventive measures recommended by the World Health Organization such as ���wearing a face mask���; ���use of hand sanitiser���; ���more frequent hand washing��� and ���increased social distancing���. They would also include an option that allows their respondents to enter any other measures that were not included in the multiple choice answers. They would label this as ���Other���. In relation to the topic of perceptions of the performance of the public health system, they would ask respondents to rate their confidence in the preparedness of various organisations to effectively deal with the COVID-19 pandemic on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not confident at all, 5 = very confident).
Before they roll out the survey, the team want to pilot the survey to check their questions are going to capture the correct information. They plan to test it internally with their colleagues and friends to ensure that the survey displays correctly for all respondents, that the questions are meaningful and make sense, and that filling it out doesn���t require more than the intended 10 minutes. A test run would allow them to rectify any issues with the survey before they release it to the public.

Introduction

Sometimes you want to be able to tease out answers from participants or take your questions in a new direction based on how a participant has responded. Surveys don���t allow this flexibility, however, interviews do. Having a personal connection with someone when conducting an interview can allow you to approach topics that are less easy to capture when self-reported, such as emotions, sensitive issues, or just being able to work out the specific terminology that a participant is using to describe a situation.

Interviews are extensively used throughout the IT sector to gain insight into the subjective experience of users, and vary in complexity, length, format and purpose. For example, they can be used for exploring the potential for a new product with a group of prospective users, or for assessing the impact of a currently deployed one. They all, however, require a ���structure��� or ���schedule��� of some form which allows the responses to be collated and analysed in a meaningful way.

Planning and conducting an interview:
 Interviews are a great way of capturing deeper understanding about a participant, and offer the opportunity to dig deeper in response to real-time responses to questions. They are however more resource intensive to deploy, and involve more work in analysing the results.

Case Study

The team in charge of improving Cortana got their market research proposal approved by the internal ethics committee at Microsoft. In the first phase of their research, they conducted observations in the nursing home. During their observations, they noticed that most senior residents rely heavily on staff to take their medication due to mobility issues. However, due to the nursing home being understaffed a lot of the time, this results in a lot of the residents being late for their medication which could cause a range of health issues. Based on these preliminary findings from the observations, the team brainstormed a few ideas on the functionalities they would add to enhance Cortana. They decided that they wanted to enhance Cortana with a smart pill dispenser. The smart pill dispenser would take the patient���s specific combination of medication and timing into account, and would broadcast a voice reminder when it���s time for them to take the medication. However, mobility is still an issue for many of the residents, and the team would like to collect further data to better understand how to mitigate this issue.
They decided to conduct interviews with the residents and staff members. Interviews are necessary in this instance to form a holistic understanding of the end users, as they allow for a flexibility that comes from the personal interaction between the team and the residents. Sarah, the team���s UX designer, was tasked with conducting the interviews. She identified 15 participants from the residents who had initially opted in to take part in the research. The participants were aged between 60 and 80 years old. They included 8 males, 7 females, and were living with varying medical conditions both chronic and temporary with varying levels of mobility. Sarah also identified 10 staff members who agreed to be interviewed. Sarah would conduct the interviews face-to-face at the nursing home, and the conversations would be audio recorded for analysis at a later stage. In consultation with the team, Sarah identified a few broad topics such as: residents' daily routines (waking time, bedtime, meals, leisure time etc.); their daily challenges and struggles with taking medication; their experiences with smart home assistants; and their degrees of mobility. She had a few guiding questions for each of the topics, but kept these questions open to allow the participants to open up and provide meaningful answers. At the start of the interviews, Sarah would start by introducing herself, asking general questions build rapport with the participant. For example, Vera was Sarah���s first interviewee. Sarah started by asking Vera about her favourite board games and what other activities she enjoyed spending time doing. The audio collected from these interviews could provide insight into potential design areas and constraints that would otherwise not have been identified by the team, but only after the audio is transcribed and analysed.

About the Workshop

Workshops are all 3 hours in length and are delivered either online using Zoom or in-person on campus. Your lecturers/tutors will have contacted you to introduce themselves and to share the Zoom link (if applicable) for the weekly workshop.

Contact the lecturer if you have any questions about the timing of a workshop or require a Zoom link for your workshop. This information is also available on Moodle.

Pre-workshop material

Each workshop is a collaborative space designed to give you a space to explore and practice new research skills. You will participate in activities that are based directly on the pre-material on this page. Before this weeks workshop you should:

Consume

You need to watch the videos (before the workshop): These aim to present content in small (5-10 minute) chunks and are subtitled.

Reflect

You should review the content: Slides and transcripts are provided for all the videos, reading these can help you understand the content in your own time.

Engage

You should engage with the associated reading: There are various resources and papers, that we'll be pointing you to before and during Week 2, so take some time to review these.

Participate

Post into the discussion board: if you have questions or insights that you want to share please don't hesitate to post these, (also if you have any questions about the unit).

Part B - Assignment Template for Week 3

For this week's assignment, you will need to plan, conduct and transcribe an interview. Complete the template below and submit to Moodle, along with your audio file and completed consent form as part of Assignment 2. Transcribing interviews that you have conducted is a key skill in conducting research. It helps you to understand nuances in the data, and expose latent topics within the data. See 
https://newmediaservices.com.au/types-of-transcription/
 for more info on types of transcription.
