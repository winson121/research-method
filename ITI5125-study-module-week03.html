WEEK 3 Roadmap — surveys & interviews

What week 3 covers, why it matters for your assignments, and the two case studies that anchor everything.

Why this week exists

Week 2 taught you to configure a study — questions, methods, participants, budget. Week 3 hands you the two instruments you will actually build for your assignments: the questionnaire/survey (Part A) and the research interview (Part B). Both look easy and both are full of traps: one badly worded question can invalidate a whole dataset, and one leading probe can bias an entire interview. The lectures, the two case studies and both workshop sheets all drill the same underlying skill — getting wording right.

The week's learning outcomes (Moodle)

Surveys — you will…
  • Understand the purpose and role of questionnaires in data collection
  • Identify and describe the different types of questionnaire questions
  • Design a questionnaire to capture specific information using best practice
  • Understand the tools available for deploying surveys
Interviews — you will…
  • Understand how interviews and focus groups capture qualitative information
  • Identify the types of interviews and focus groups that can be arranged
  • Critique interviews for good and bad practice
  • Plan an interview appropriate for a topic
  • Describe the characteristics of interviews in research

Case study 1 · Daniela's COVID survey (Part A)

Daniela, a data scientist at an IT consultancy, is commissioned by the government to design a digital platform for public-health communications about COVID-19. There is no existing data on whether people changed behaviour in response to communications, and the pandemic is moving fast — so the team runs a large-scale survey through Facebook (Facebook Insights to reach people across states/territories; Ads Manager campaigns to recruit respondents). Because Facebook respondents may not represent the wider population, the survey opens with a demographics section (age, sex, education). They fix 4 topics: (1) underlying medical conditions, (2) adoption of preventive behaviours, (3) perception of the public-health system's response, (4) awareness of current public-health advice. Design decisions: keep it under 10 minutes; prioritise closed multiple-choice over open-ended; use 5-point Likert scales for confidence ratings (1 = not confident at all … 5 = very confident); offer WHO-based options for preventive behaviours (face mask, hand sanitiser, more frequent hand washing, increased social distancing) plus “Other”; and pilot internally with colleagues and friends first to check display, meaningfulness and timing before the public launch.

Why it matters: this case is the template for Workshop 3A and for your own survey work — topics → question forms → scales → categories → pilot → launch.

Case study 2 · Cortana smart pill dispenser (Part B)

Microsoft's team improving Cortana gets ethics approval to research a smart pill dispenser for a nursing home. Observations show most senior residents rely on staff for medication; understaffing means medication is often late. The dispenser would broadcast a voice reminder at the right time — but mobility is still an issue, so the team interviews residents and staff. Sarah (UX designer) interviews 15 residents (opted in, aged 60–80, 8 male / 7 female, varied chronic and temporary conditions and mobility levels) and 10 staff, face-to-face at the nursing home, audio-recorded. Broad topics: daily routines; daily challenges with medication; experience with smart-home assistants; degrees of mobility. Guiding questions stay open; she builds rapport first (Vera is asked about her favourite board games). Key line: the audio only becomes insight after transcription and analysis.

Why it matters: this is the anatomy of a real interview study — and it reappears in the quiz as detail questions (numbers, roles, why interviews were chosen).

WEEK 3 · PART A Surveys & questionnaires

Everything from the Part A slides, the SurveyMonkey reading and the Daniela case — the concepts the quiz tests.

Survey vs questionnaire — the difference that keeps coming up

SurveyQuestionnaire
The broader term — it describes the content, the method and the analysis. The whole research process: designing and distributing the questions, collecting, aggregating and analysing responses.A written set of questions — just the instrument, the form itself. Every survey contains a questionnaire, but a questionnaire can exist alone (e.g. a signup form).
Includes: individual questions ✓ · delivery of the questionnaire ✓ · analysis of responses ✓Includes: individual questions ✓ · delivery ✗ · analysis ✗
Exam-ready line: a questionnaire is the set of written questions; a survey is that set plus the entire process around it (design → distribution → collection → aggregation → analysis). The lecture cites surveymonkey.com/mp/survey-vs-questionnaire.

What information a survey can obtain

The slides list six kinds of information: facts & knowledge (biography) opinions / beliefs attitudes motives actions (past behaviour) likely future behaviour. Notice the design consequence: each kind wants a different question form — you ask actions with frequency scales, attitudes with Likert scales, and you only get at motives well with open or carefully framed questions.

When is a questionnaire appropriate?

All four conditions from the slides:
  1. We have a good idea of what to expect (the space is mapped enough to pre-write options).
  2. We have (a) literate respondents, or (b) time to administer it to them (otherwise they can't self-complete).
  3. We are interested in counts, frequencies — quantitative patterns across many people.
  4. We want to generalise to the larger population.

If instead you need to explore the unknown, dig into why, or handle sensitive/emotional territory, that is when interviews (Part B) take over.

Types of survey study — cross-sectional vs longitudinal

DesignWhat it isTrade-off
Cross-sectionalOne questionnaire, one point in time — a snapshot of the population. Daniela's COVID survey is the model case.Cheap, fast; but can't show change over time or cause–effect ordering.
LongitudinalThe same people re-surveyed repeatedly (a panel) or different samples drawn from the same population over time (trend study).Shows change and can establish what came first; expensive, slow, suffers panel attrition (people drop out).
Quiz trap: “one snapshot of many people” = cross-sectional; “same people followed across waves” = longitudinal (panel). A cohort surveyed once a year for four years is longitudinal, not four cross-sectionals.

Deployment — the tools a survey can ride on

The options (the learning outcome explicitly asks for these):
  • Online survey platforms — Qualtrics (Monash's own platform; the workshop's mock survey runs on it), SurveyMonkey, Google Forms: build, distribute by link, auto-aggregate. Best for literate self-completing respondents at scale.
  • Social-media recruitment + platform — Daniela's pattern: Facebook Insights to scope the audience, Ads Manager campaigns to recruit, survey link to capture. Reach is huge; representativeness is not — hence the demographics section.
  • Email / mailing lists — direct links to known populations (students, customers); watch self-selection bias in who clicks.
  • Face-to-face or phone administration — a trained person reads the questionnaire aloud and records answers; the slide's condition (b) “time to administer (if not literate)”. Expensive per response, but reaches anyone.
  • Paper / intercept — clinics, street intercepts, lecture-theatre handouts; works offline; data entry afterwards is the cost.

Rule of thumb: self-completion online when you have literate respondents and a good frame; administered (human-read) when you don't.

Sampling — how respondents get chosen

The slides name two approaches; the quiz (and every methods unit) expects the full menu. Both families below are fair game:

ApproachMechanismWatch out for
Simple random samplingEvery member of the population has an equal chance of selection (drawn from a complete list).Needs a sampling frame (list); slow to build big n.
Systematic samplingOrder the frame, pick a random start, then take every k-th member (e.g. every 25th student).Dangerous if the list has a hidden pattern with the same period as k (e.g. every 25th happens to be an international student).
Stratified samplingSplit the population into subgroups (strata) that matter (faculty, age, sex), then random-sample within every stratum — usually in proportion to size.Needs to know each member's stratum in advance; guarantees representation, not automatic generalisability.
Cluster samplingSplit the population into natural groups (clusters) — tutorial classes, suburbs — randomly pick whole clusters, survey everyone inside them.Cheaper to run (fewer sites); less precise because people in a cluster resemble each other.
ApproachMechanismWatch out for
Convenience samplingWhoever is easiest to reach — your class, your feed, passers-by.Cheap and instant; almost never representative. Daniela's Facebook ads were effectively this.
Snowball samplingExisting respondents identify more respondents — recruitment chains through networks.Biased toward connected/visible members; the go-to method for hidden or hard-to-reach groups.
Purposive (judgemental) samplingResearcher hand-picks participants believed to be informative — experts, key informants, extreme cases.Depends entirely on the researcher's judgement; no statistical generalisation.
Quota samplingFix target numbers per subgroup (e.g. 50 domestic + 50 international) and fill the quotas with whoever is available.Looks like stratified but is non-probability — the filling is convenience, so the sample can still skew within quotas.
Voluntary / self-selection samplingPeople choose themselves in — link posts, “click here to give your opinion”.The SETU pattern: the keen and the annoyed over-answer; silent majority invisible.
How to choose: do you have a frame (a list of the population)? → probability family (random / systematic / stratified / cluster — stratify when subgroup balance matters, cluster when travel is the cost). No frame, or a hidden group? → non-probability family (convenience is fastest, snowball reaches the hidden, purposive targets expertise, quota forces rough balance, voluntary is cheapest and most biased). Whichever you use: collect demographics and report the bias — that is Daniela's lesson.

Sources cited on the slide: statpac.com/surveys/sampling.htm; the extended family is standard research-methods taxonomy your quiz draws on. Daniela's team effectively used a convenience/volunteer channel (Facebook ads) — which is exactly why they front-loaded a demographics section: when the channel isn't representative, you measure the skew so you can read results by segment.

Response-rate physics (SurveyMonkey reading — real numbers)

  • SurveyMonkey deploys 3.5 million+ surveys a year.
  • Questionnaires with 40 questions have ~10% lower response rates than 10-question ones.
  • People spend ~10 minutes on a questionnaire regardless of its length — so extra questions don't get extra time, they get less attention per question.
  • Therefore: fewer, sharper questions = more thought per answer. This is why Daniela's team capped the survey at 10 minutes and prioritised closed questions.

Question types — how the whole taxonomy fits together

First split: what is the question measuring?
ClassCapturesTypical forms
Factual / demographicWho the respondent is and what is true of them (age band, faculty, enrolment status)Banded ranges, single-choice categories
BehaviouralWhat they actually do / did (actions, past behaviour, likely future behaviour)Frequency scales, numeric bands, checklists
AttitudinalWhat they think and feel (opinions, beliefs, attitudes, motives)Likert, semantic differential, ranking, open-ended
Second split: how is it answered?

Closed — fixed options the respondent picks from (dichotomous, multiple-choice, multi-select, Likert, semantic differential, ranking, banded numeric, matrix). Fast to answer and analyse; requires you to already know the answer space. Open — free text. Slow and expensive to analyse; reaches motives and the unexpected. Daniela's closed-first rule exists because her team needed timely counts — open questions were rationed, not banned.

Scale levels to name-drop: nominal categories with no order (faculty, ethnicity) · ordinal ordered categories without equal gaps (Never → Very often; Likert points) · interval/ratio numbers where differences are meaningful (hours, kg, WAM). The level decides what statistics are legal later — you can average hours (ratio) but not faculty codes (nominal).

The ten classic wording pitfalls (the logic behind Workshop 3A Activity 1)

Every flawed question in the workshop sheet is an instance of one of these patterns. Learn the pattern, not the example — the quiz swaps in new examples:

PitfallHow it breaks your dataFix pattern
1 · Leading / loaded wordingTelegraphs the “right” answer; measures agreement with you, not the respondent's view.Strip value words & prestige appeals; state both sides neutrally.
2 · Double-barrelledTwo questions fused; a single answer can't represent both → uninterpretable.Split into one ask per question.
3 · Double negative“Agree that students should not…” flips logic; respondents misread.Affirmative stem + balanced agree–disagree scale.
4 · Vague / undefined terms“Young people”, “these days”, “often” mean different things to different respondents.Define population, timeframe, unit.
5 · Presumptuous / screening failureAssumes facts (a son, a theft) not established; alienates respondents.Screen question first; neutral conditional phrasing.
6 · Unanswerable (respondent can't know)Asks for estimates of others' behaviour or unknowable totals → garbage numbers.Ask about the respondent's own behaviour in a bounded period.
7 · Sensitive without safeguardsIncome, ethnicity, cheating → refusal or lying; non-response bias.Banded ranges, “Prefer not to say”, explain why it's asked.
8 · Overlapping / non-exhaustive optionsTwo bands both fit (or none fit) → forced wrong answers.Mutually exclusive + collectively exhaustive + “Other”.
9 · Unbalanced scale / unordered labelsSkewed midpoints or scrambled frequency labels distort distributions.Symmetric anchors in logical order.
10 · Scope too big“What can be done about global warming?” → unanalysable essays.Narrow to one aspect with structured options.

WEEK 3 · PART A Workshop 3A · solved — Surveys & Questionnaires

The studio sheet has 3 activities: question critique, developing a questionnaire, and peer feedback. Below: the reusable template for each, then multiple complete model answers with the reasoning made explicit, so you can speak to any of them in class.

Activity 1 · Question critique (Parts A & B)

The template — critique any survey question in 4 moves
  1. Name the flaw(s) — map the wording onto the pitfall taxonomy from the Surveys section (leading, double-barrelled, double negative, vague terms, presumptuous, unanswerable, sensitive-without-safeguards, overlapping options, unbalanced scale, oversized scope).
  2. Show how it breaks the data — what would respondents actually do (guess, lie, abandon, answer a different question) and what that does to your analysis.
  3. State the rewrite rule — the general pattern that fixes this class of flaw.
  4. Write the improved question — one concrete rewrite, with its response options.

Part C is presenting this back to another group: group your findings by pitfall, lead with the two worst questions, and give the one-line fix for each. (The sheet also asks you to complete a mock Qualtrics survey in 5–10 minutes — that's you experiencing a real instrument from the respondent's side; notice which questions felt awkward and why.)

How the pattern logic applies here: every model answer below follows the same 4 moves. The diagnosis references pitfall numbers from the taxonomy table; the rewrite applies that pitfall's fix pattern. If you can reproduce this move sequence for a brand-new flawed question, you can handle any critique the quiz or the class throws at you.

Q1 · “What is wrong with young people these days and what can we do about it?”

Flaws: double-barrelled (two questions: what's wrong + what to do); loaded negative presupposition (“what is wrong” assumes something is); vague terms (“young people”, “these days”); oversized scope (pitfalls 1, 2, 4, 10).

Why it breaks the data: respondents who disagree that anything is wrong have no honest answer; those who answer are answering different questions; “young people” could mean 15-year-olds or 35-year-olds, so answers aren't comparable.

Rewrite rule: split the asks, define the population with an age band and the period with a timeframe, remove the presupposition.

Improved version A (opinion, defined terms): “Thinking about people aged 18–25 today, how much do you agree or disagree with each of the following? — They have good job prospects / They show respect to others / They are optimistic about the future. (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (behaviour, defined terms): “In the past 12 months, how often have people under 25 in your local area done anything that you found disruptive? (Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Frequently)” — notice the loaded “what is wrong” becomes a neutral, observable behaviour with a bounded timeframe.

Q2 · “Do you go swimming? [1] Never, [2] Rarely, [3] Frequently, [4] Sometimes”

Flaws: unordered frequency labels (Sometimes listed after Frequently — scales must run low→high); vague frequency words with no anchors; stem/scale mismatch (a yes/no stem offered a frequency scale) (pitfalls 4, 8, 9).

Why it breaks the data: category order scrambles the underlying continuum, so the data can't be ranked or even consistently coded; “frequently” means weekly to one respondent and daily to another.

Rewrite rule: put response categories in logical order, anchor the vague ends, and make the stem match the scale.

Improved version A (ordered labels): “How often do you go swimming? (Never / Less than once a month / 1–3 times a month / At least once a week)”

Improved version B (numeric bands): “In a typical month, how many times do you go swimming? (0 / 1–2 / 3–5 / 6 or more)” — numeric bands remove all ambiguity about what “frequently” means.

Q3 · “What do you think can be done about global warming?”

Flaws: oversized unbounded open question; “what do you think” invites essays; answers will be incomparable and unanalysable at any sample size (pitfalls 4, 10).

Why it breaks the data: you'd get 200 unique essays you can't aggregate; a survey exists to produce counts and frequencies, so the question defeats the instrument.

Rewrite rule: narrow to one aspect and give a structured response; if depth is needed, use one bounded open question or switch method to interviews.

Improved version A (forced priority): “Which ONE of these would do most to reduce Australia's emissions? (More renewable energy / Better public transport / Stricter building standards / Reducing meat consumption / None of these / Don't know)”

Improved version B (own behaviour): “Which of these have you done in the past 12 months to reduce your environmental impact? Select all that apply. (Reduced car use / Reduced meat or dairy / Reduced flying / Switched to renewable energy / None)”

Q4 · “Most medical professionals think that smoking causes lung cancer; do you: Strongly agree … Strongly disagree”

Flaws: leading via authority/bandwagon appeal — the preamble pressures agreement and turns the item into a test of whether you'll side with doctors (pitfall 1).

Why it breaks the data: social desirability and prestige cues inflate agreement; you no longer measure the respondent's belief, you measure their reluctance to contradict experts.

Rewrite rule: delete every prestige preamble; ask the respondent's own view with a balanced stem.

Improved version A (belief, balanced): “To what extent do you agree or disagree: Smoking causes lung cancer. (Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither / Agree / Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (evidence strength): “In your view, how strong is the evidence that smoking causes lung cancer? (No evidence / Weak / Moderate / Strong / Very strong)” — this separates belief from perceived evidence, which is usually what the researcher actually wants.

Q5 · “Do you agree that students should not have to take an exam at the end of their course? [a] Yes; [b] No.”

Flaws: double negative (agree + “should not”) — cognitive load flips the logic; acquiescence bias from the “Do you agree” stem; yes/no collapses all nuance (pitfalls 1, 3, 9).

Why it breaks the data: many respondents will answer the opposite of what they mean; Yes/No also hides strength of feeling, so a 51–49 split tells you nothing about intensity.

Rewrite rule: make the statement affirmative, use a balanced agree–disagree scale, never ask “do you agree”.

Improved version A (Likert): “Students should have to take an exam at the end of their course. (Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither / Agree / Strongly agree)”

Improved version B (forced choice between positions): “Which statement comes closer to your view? A: Every student should sit a final exam. B: Students should be able to complete a course without a final exam. (A / B / Neither)”

Q6 · “Has your son ever stolen anything? If so when?”

Flaws: presumptuous screening failure (assumes you have a son); sensitive and accusatory (implying theft by your child); compound conditional demanding a precise date for a stigmatised act; no refusal option (pitfalls 5, 7).

Why it breaks the data: childless respondents can't answer; parents of daughters are excluded; those who can answer face a threat — expect refusals, lies or abandoned surveys (non-response bias exactly on the sensitive item).

Rewrite rule: screen first, neutralise the language, bound the timeframe, always offer “Prefer not to say”.

Improved version A (screen + neutral + bounded): “Do you have any children? (No / Yes — son(s) only / Yes — daughter(s) only / Yes — both)” → If yes: “Thinking about the past 12 months, has any of your children taken something from a shop or person without permission? (Yes / No / Prefer not to say)”

Improved version B (non-accusatory framing): “Has your child ever been spoken to at school or by another family about taking things that weren't theirs? (Yes / No / Not applicable / Prefer not to say)”

Q7 · “What do you think about the left-wing media's attempt to blackmail the Government?”

Flaws: heavily loaded political framing (“left-wing”, “blackmail”); asserts unverified facts as premise; merges actor + action + evaluation into one ask (pitfalls 1, 2, 4).

Why it breaks the data: respondents who reject the premise can't answer honestly; the item measures agreement with the framing, not media perceptions; politically, half your sample will shut down.

Rewrite rule: replace value-laden nouns with neutral referents, split actor from action from evaluation, never embed disputed facts in the stem.

Improved version A (trust battery): “How much do you trust each of the following to report political news accurately? (TV broadcasters / Newspapers / Online-only outlets / Social media) (No trust / Little / Moderate / High)”

Improved version B (neutral perception): “How much do you agree or disagree: Recent coverage of the Government by Australian media has been fair. (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree)” — one claim, neutral wording, balanced scale.

Q8 · “How much food do you think the average family throws away in a week?”

Flaws: unanswerable — the respondent cannot know what the “average family” discards; unit undefined (“how much” = kg? litres? dollars?); invites a wild guess (pitfalls 4, 6, 8).

Why it breaks the data: you'd collect confident-sounding fiction; two respondents with identical households give different answers because they guessed in different units.

Rewrite rule: ask about the respondent's own behaviour in a bounded period, define the unit, offer a “Not sure”.

Improved version A (own household, unit given): “Roughly how much food does YOUR household throw away in a typical week? (None / Less than 1 kg / 1–2 kg / 3–5 kg / More than 5 kg / Not sure)”

Improved version B (behaviour proxy): “In the past week, how often did your household throw away food that was left over or had gone off? (Never / Once / 2–3 times / 4+ times)” — frequency of disposal is estimable; kilograms of “average families” are not.

Q9 · “How much do you earn?”

Flaws: sensitive item with no safeguards: exact figure demanded, no bands, no refusal option (pitfall 7).

Why it breaks the data: expect refusals, rounding chaos and survey abandonment right at the item — precisely the people who refuse are often those you most need, biasing every cross-tab that uses income.

Rewrite rule: band it, make it skippable, and mark it optional unless essential.

Improved version A (bands + refusal): “Which of the following best describes your annual income before tax? (Under $20,000 / $20,000–$39,999 / $40,000–$59,999 / $60,000–$99,999 / $100,000 or more / Prefer not to say)”

Improved version B (optional, broad bands): “Roughly where does your yearly income fall? (Below average / About average / Above average / Prefer not to say)” — coarse but honest when the precise bands would scare respondents away.

Q10 · “What is your ethnicity?”

Flaws: sensitive with no category list, no self-describe escape, no refusal option; free-text ethnicity is nearly impossible to aggregate consistently (pitfalls 4, 7, 8).

Why it breaks the data: respondents write “Australian”, “Chinese-Malaysian”, “mixed” — each defensible, none comparable; some will abandon rather than answer.

Rewrite rule: use the standard classification of the study country (for Australia, the ABS standard), add “Other (please specify)” and “Prefer not to say”, and state why the data is collected.

Improved version A (single choice, standard list): “Which of the following best describes your ethnic background? (Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander / North-West European / Southern and Eastern European / North African and Middle Eastern / South-East Asian / North-East Asian / Southern and Central Asian / Peoples of the Americas / Sub-Saharan African / Pacific Islander / Other (please specify) / Prefer not to say)”

The template — how to develop topics
  1. Test breadth: can you immediately imagine 4+ distinct questions for it? If not, it's a question, not a topic.

WEEK 3 · PART B Research interviews

Everything from the Part B slides — the “interview society”, epistemology & ontology, advantages/disadvantages, interview types — plus the Cortana case study and the craft rules the workshop tests.

We live in an “interview society”

David Silverman (1993) coined it: interviews are everywhere — online/TV/radio news, talk shows and magazines, documentaries, job seeking. Interviews were once the domain of professional practice; now they “pervade and produce our contemporary cultural experiences and knowledge of our authentic personal, private selves”.

Why researchers care: research interviews leverage the fact that people already know how to “play the game” of presenting themselves through interviews. That cultural fluency is what makes the method work — and also what makes people rehearsed, so a good interviewer works to get past the performance.

Before you interview — the questions to answer first

  1. What is your research topic? What do you wish to explore or explain?
  2. What are your research questions?
  3. What is the purpose of your research?
  4. What might represent knowledge or evidence?

Then two philosophical checkpoints: your epistemological position — epistemology is the study of what does and does not constitute your knowledge; and the ontology of what you study — the concepts and categories in a subject area, their properties and relations. Ontology changes the questions: “sexism” as a study of attitudes asks different questions than sexism as a study of discourse or of institutions.

Advantages of interviews (from the slides)

What interviews win you
  • Flexible — the line of inquiry is modifiable as you learn
  • Benefits from non-verbal communication
  • Gets “behind” the immediate response — the why, versus a questionnaire's what
  • Usable with relatively little prior knowledge of a situation (the typical survey first needs exploratory qualitative work)
  • Situational — captures the participant's view of the world
  • Engaging — often viewed positively by participants
What interviews cost you
  • Not standardised — for semi-structured and unstructured forms
  • Not replicable — for any form of interview
  • Time-consuming (therefore expensive) — typically face-to-face
  • Recruitment can be challenging — time commitment on the participant's side
  • Analysis takes time and effort — transcription, qualitative analysis, disseminating

Types of interview

TypeWho / structureBest when
Structured (respondent interview)Rigid style; researcher has a comprehensive list of questions; no need for follow-up interviewsYou know exactly what you need and want every participant asked identically
Semi-structured (respondent interview)A protocol guides the interview; can incorporate conversational aspects; lots of flexibilityYou have topic areas but want to dig deeper on answers — the default for UX research
Unstructured (informer interview)Few if any prepared questions; progresses like a normal conversation about the research topicSensitive topics; participants' narratives may redirect the conversation away from aspects you anticipated

Respondent vs informer: respondent interviews ask a participant about themselves; informer interviews ask a key informant about a situation or organisation. Unstructured interviews pair naturally with informers because the informer steers.

Conduct rules (what the observer is told to watch for in Workshop 3B)
  • Ask, then listen — follow up on answers before moving on; don't robotically march through the schedule.
  • Use the probes you planned — they exist to elicit detail, not to interrogate.
  • One question at a time — no double-barrelled spoken questions either.
  • Neutral reactions — no nodding-along, frowning or “good answer!”; you bias the next answer.
  • Tolerate silence — let the participant think; filling pauses kills reflection.
  • Record with consent — and say what happens to the data before you start (the workshop's own rule).
  • Keep the interviewee talking ~80% of the time — if you're talking more, you're interviewing yourself.

Activity 1 · Interview topics

The brief (from the sheet): plan an interview on “Grocery Shopping and Technology” — how has technology transformed the way people do grocery shopping? The focus must be narrowed through group discussion: consider families / travel / retail / work as lenses, and decide whether you talk about a single specific country or all countries. Deliverable: 5 mini-topics, each with a name, a brief description, and why you plan to ask about it. The template — from giant focus to 5 mini-topics
  1. Pick the lens (one line, group agreement): population + context — e.g. “Australian households with children, everyday weekly shopping, last 2 years”. This is the narrowing the sheet demands.
  2. Brainstorm the whole space (10 minutes, no judging): planning, in-store, online/delivery, payment, budgeting, loyalty, trust, family roles…
  3. Cluster into 4–6 candidate mini-topics — each must be a theme you could ask 4+ questions about, and together they should cover the lens without overlap.
  4. Name, describe, justify each: name = 2–4 words; description = what it covers; why = what it reveals about “how technology transformed grocery shopping”.
How the pattern logic applies: “how has technology transformed grocery shopping?” is unanswerable as one question — it's an area of concern, like week 3A's “adjusting to student life”. The lens converts it into a bounded population and timeframe; the mini-topics partition the space so that each interview question has an obvious home.

Activity 2 · Interview schedule (10 minutes)

The template — building the schedule
  1. Select 2 mini-topics from Activity 1 (the sheet says 2 — choose ones that flow into each other, e.g. planning → online shopping).
  2. Write 5–6 questions per topic using a variety of question types: at least one opening/rapport, one grand tour, structural follow-ups, one contrast, and probes under every question (“include potential probes for each question, to elicit a more detailed response”).

  5. Record justifications: for each question (why + type), for each probe (what detail it elicits), and for the order (why this sequence).

Talk ratio…talked ≤ 20% of the time; the interviewee did most of the talking

WEEK 3 · DEEP DIVE The interviewer's craft — probes, focus groups, sensitive topics

Everything on this page sits one level below the interview-types material: not which interview to run, but how to run it well once the participant is in the chair. These are the standard qualitative-interviewing canon (Spradley 1979; Kvale & Brinkmann), and the quiz draws scenario questions straight from it.

Types of probes

A probe is a short follow-up that invites more of the participant's meaning without adding any of yours. Five standard forms:

ProbeSounds likeWhat it buys you
Elaboration probe“Tell me more about that.” · “What happened next?”Expansion of whatever the participant just said; keeps the thread moving with zero new content from you.
Clarification probe“What do you mean by ‘falls over’?”Pins down the participant's own term so you code it faithfully instead of guessing.
Exemplification probe“Can you give an example?”Grounds an abstract claim in one concrete episode, which is where the analysable detail lives.
Echo probeRepeat their last few words as a question: “The list writes itself?”Signals listening and hands the turn back; asserts nothing at all.
Summary probe“So would it be fair to say…?”Checks your interpretation before moving on, and gives the participant a chance to correct you. The safety net against mis-hearing.
The rule that ties all five together: probes elicit detail without steering. The moment your follow-up asserts an interpretation, demands agreement or embeds an expected answer, it stops being a probe and becomes a leading question. This distinction is the single most quizzed point in this section.

Focus groups

The week-3 configurations table lists the focus group as the group-based interview configuration (alongside one-to-one, debate-based and multiple-interviewer setups). One moderator, a small recruited group, one semi-structured guide.

Why the interaction is the data: eight individual interviews give you eight separate accounts of what people say they think. A focus group lets you watch participants challenge, agree, joke, defer and persuade each other in real time, so group norms, consensus-seeking and social influence surface live rather than being reported second-hand. You are studying the conversation, not just harvesting eight shorter interviews at once.

The moderator's role is to facilitate, not dominate: set ground rules up front, keep dominant voices in check, and actively draw out quiet members (a direct invitation or round-robin turn is the standard move). The moderator's own opinions stay out of the room, because one expressed view reframes every answer that follows.

Practical norms: typically 6–10 participants, 60–90 minutes, and a semi-structured guide so the session stays comparable across groups while leaving room to chase what the group raises.

Choosing the configuration
Choose…When…
SurveyYou have a good idea of what to expect and want counts, frequencies and generalisation to a population.
One-to-one interviewYou need depth on individual experience, sensitive territory, or the participant's own terminology; privacy matters.
Focus groupThe research question is about shared norms, consensus or how people influence each other; the exchange between participants is itself the evidence.

Sensitive topics

Income, health, medication dependence, cheating: sensitive questions can be asked, but only with deliberate technique:

  • Place them late in the schedule, after easy warm-up and substantive questions have built the rapport you will spend here.
  • Use permissive framing that normalises both answers: “Some people find bills pile up, others don't. What fits your situation?”
  • State the right to skip explicitly, out loud, before the sensitive block.
  • Never press. A pause or a look away is a skip signal. Offer a graceful exit and move on.
  • React neutrally to whatever comes back: no wincing, no praise, no surprise. Your reaction is data about what is acceptable to say.
The slides connect this to interview type: unstructured interviews suit sensitive topics, because with no rigid schedule there is nothing forcing the question at a fixed moment. The conversation can approach, retreat and return when the participant is ready.

Common interviewer errors

ErrorWhy it corrupts the dataCorrective habit
LeadingMeasures compliance with your expectation, not the participant's view.Neutral stems; put your hypotheses in the analysis, not the question.
Double-barrelled spoken questionsTwo questions fused into one; a single answer cannot represent both halves.Write the schedule down and ask one thing at a time.
InterruptingTrains the participant to give short, safe answers and cuts off the interesting half of sentences.Take notes on what to probe and wait for the turn to end.
Filling silencesThe best material often arrives after a pause; your filler replaces it.Count to five silently before speaking.

Remote / online interviews

Pros
  • Lower cost: no travel, no venue, easier scheduling.
  • Recording built into the platform (with consent).
  • Wider reach: participants anywhere, including mobility-limited groups such as the Cortana residents.
Cons
  • Lost non-verbal channel, which the slides list as a face-to-face advantage; rapport is harder.
  • Connectivity failures and audio dropout corrupt the recording.
  • Home environments bring privacy limits on sensitive topics.
Mitigations: run a quick tech check before the session, agree a backup phone number or re-booking rule, ask for camera-on where privacy allows, send the consent form digitally in advance, and keep probes extra explicit since the nod-and-smile channel is gone.

Drill 6 · The two classic contrast traps

Stratified vs Cluster

Both probability. Stratified: sample from every stratum → guarantees subgroup representation. Cluster: sample whole groups, only chosen ones surveyed → cheaper fieldwork, lower precision. Memory hook: strata = spread across all; cluster = clump into some.

Stratified vs Quota

Both care about subgroups. Stratified: random selection within every stratum — probability. Quota: targets filled by whoever is available — non-probability. Memory hook: stratified = drawn, quota = grabbed.
